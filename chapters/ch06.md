# Chapter 6 — Lucifer

The fence gave way to a road, and the road gave way to the town.

The orchard's shade was gone the moment the trees were. Heat sat over the street instead, thick and unmoving, pressing up off the asphalt in a shimmer that blurred the far end of the block. Lucifer noted it the way he noted everything here: present, catalogued, set aside.

"Main Street," James said, picking the basket back up. "Such as it is."

"That's the whole name?" Claire fell into step beside him. "Main Street?"

"It's accurate."

The street ran two blocks of storefronts before it widened into an open green at the center of town — grass, a scatter of benches, a fountain that had stopped working sometime before any of the four of them arrived and hadn't been fixed since. Beyond the green, a coffee shop with a hand-lettered sign and its door propped open against the heat.

"That's the good coffee," Claire said, pointing without slowing. "Everyone in town has an opinion about it, which tells you the whole coffee situation here."

"I have an opinion about it," James said.

"Your opinion is that it's coffee."

"That's a valid opinion."

An older man sweeping the step outside a shop they passed looked up long enough to nod at James and Claire, then let his eyes go once over Lucifer and Azrael before he went back to sweeping — new faces, noted, nothing more made of it yet.

Lucifer catalogued him back and moved on.

"That's the park," James said, nodding at the green. "Half the town ends up there eventually. There's a woman who runs a painting class out of it on weekends, if that's ever useful information to you specifically." He said it lightly, not really expecting an answer.

"It won't be," Lucifer said.

Azrael didn't correct him. He also didn't look convinced.

They cut past the green rather than through it, James and Claire trading the names of shops without really looking at them anymore — the shoe repair that had been closed longer than it had been open, the church whose bell rang ten minutes fast on purpose because the pastor liked being early, the school lot standing empty for the summer. Lucifer filed each without comment, watching the street for the one detail that would not belong here. Nothing did. Ordinary life went on being ordinary in every direction — a woman arguing cheerfully with a delivery driver over a crate, two boys trading a bicycle back and forth down the block, none of it aimed at him, none of it requiring anything from him.

"You're doing the thing," Claire said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you look at everyone like you're filling out a form about them."

"I'm not filling out a form."

"You're doing the face of a person filling out a form."

Lucifer didn't answer that. Claire didn't push it — filed, same as the rest.

Ten minutes past the green, the shops thinned into houses, and the houses thinned into fewer of them, better spaced, with yards old enough to have trees planted on purpose. James slowed in front of one with a covered porch and turned up the front walk without breaking stride, already familiar with it.

"This is her," he said.

The porch light hung dark in its fixture, the socket empty enough that Lucifer could see straight through the glass to nothing behind it. A new bulb sat on the porch step beside the mat, still in its cardboard sleeve.

Claire climbed the two steps first. "She's probably home. Sam's car's gone, hers isn't."

"You know their cars," Lucifer said.

"I know everyone's cars. It's a small town." Claire raised her hand to knock.

Azrael's eyes went once over the house — the dead light, the walk, the door — the same catalogue Lucifer had just run, arriving at nothing different.

Claire knocked.
