# Breakfast — Elizabeth

Elizabeth came down the stairs in the yellow floral dress she'd pulled off the top of the closet without much thought — small white flowers scattered over a pale yellow background, thin straps, a hem that hit just above her knee. It was soft from too many washes, one of those dresses that had stopped being new so long ago it had become a kind of default. She'd tied her hair back on the way out of her room and left it at that.

Halfway down the stairs the smell reached her — butter, and under it the particular smoky sweetness of sausage in a pan. Sam was up, then, and already well into it.

She followed the smell into the kitchen. Sam stood at the stove in yesterday's t-shirt, a spatula in one hand, the small radio on the counter turned down low enough that it was mostly just rhythm and no words.

"Morning," he said without turning around. "Sleep okay?"

"Good," she said, and dropped into her usual chair at the table, pressing two fingers to her forehead and rubbing slow circles there.

He turned then, plate in hand, and set eggs and sausage down in front of her. "You've got—" He stopped, leaned in a little, squinting. "You've got a cut. Right there." He pointed at his own eyebrow to show her where.

She reached up and touched it — a thin, tender line just above her left brow, already starting to close over. She hadn't looked in a mirror this morning, hadn't thought about it at all until his finger was pointing at his own face like a map.

"Oh." She prodded it once more, testing. "Yeah. I lost a fight with the table last night."

Sam laughed, short and surprised, and went back to the stove for his own plate. "The table won."

"The table always wins. It doesn't move."

"That's usually the secret to winning, yeah." He sat down across from her, spun his fork once in his fingers, and nodded at her forehead again. "Does it hurt?"

"No." She picked up her own fork. "Barely felt it happen, honestly. Just sort of noticed it after."

"Very reassuring. Great data point for future table-related incidents."

"I'll write it up."

He let it go, the way he usually let things go once she'd waved a hand at them, and for a while they just ate — eggs, sausage, the radio humming under everything. She could feel the cut when she moved her eyebrow too much, a small pinch, nothing worth thinking about further.

"So," Sam said eventually, around a bite, "how are you feeling about all the college stuff? You've been quiet about it."

"I have not been quiet about it."

"You've mentioned it maybe twice. In two weeks."

"That's a normal amount of mentioning."

He raised an eyebrow at her — the unscarred one — and waited.

"I'm good," she said. "Really. I finished the housing form last week, I told you that. I'm just waiting on the roommate assignment thing, they said it'd be out by the end of the month."

"And the — what was it, the orientation stuff? Did you register for a session yet?"

"I did the early one. The one in three weeks." She pushed a piece of sausage around her plate. "I picked the earliest date so I wouldn't have to think about it hanging out there all August."

"Smart." He took a sip of his coffee. "You nervous?"

"About orientation? No."

"About the whole thing."

She shrugged, kept her eyes on her plate a beat longer than the shrug needed. "It's fine. It's just a lot of forms right now. It doesn't feel real yet, it just feels like paperwork."

"It'll feel real when you're standing in a dorm room with somebody else's furniture."

"Cool, thank you, very comforting, I'll think about that every day until then."

"That's what I'm here for." He grinned at her over his coffee. "Emotional preparation."

"You're doing a great job."

"I know." He forked up more eggs. "You'd tell me, though. If you were actually freaking out about it."

"I'm not actually freaking out about it," she said, and meant it enough that it came out easy, no catch in it at all. "It's good. I'm good."

He nodded, satisfied enough, and let the subject go again. She was glad of it — not because there was anything under it worth hiding exactly, just that she didn't feel like handing him one more thing to carry when he already carried plenty without complaining about it.

It was a few minutes later, reaching past him for the pepper, that she noticed the new postcard on the fridge — held up under the same chipped magnet as always, a glossy rectangle that hadn't been there yesterday.

"When did that show up?" She nodded at it.

Sam glanced over his shoulder. "Yesterday's mail."

"Where's it from?"

"London."

She waited a second for more, and when nothing came, prompted him. "That's it? What's it say?"

"That's the whole thing." He reached over, pulled it off the fridge, and slid it across the table to her.

She turned it over. A photograph of a red phone booth on the front, worn at the corners the way all of them were by the time they arrived. On the back, in familiar cramped handwriting, just a handful of words and no more — nothing that answered any question beyond the one it was already answering.

"That's all it says," she said, half a question.

"That's all it says." Sam took it back and pinned it under the magnet again, next to the others already crowding that corner of the fridge. "You know how it goes."

She did know how it went, so she let it sit there and went back to her plate, scraping up the last of the eggs.

She finished before he did — she almost always did, some race she wasn't trying to win — and sat back, tapping her fork once against the empty plate.

"I'm heading to the store later," she said. "You need anything?"

Sam thought about it for a second, still chewing. "Milk. We're basically out. Eggs, since we just used the rest of them. Uh — bread, if there's a loaf that isn't the weird seedy kind."

"No promises."

"And," he added, pointing his fork at her, "if you're going to the cafe with Soph, grab me a chocolate croissant."

She paused with her own fork halfway to the sink. "How do you know I'm going to the cafe with Sophie?"

"Because you always go to the cafe with Sophie."

"That's not — I could be doing something else today."

"Are you doing something else today?"

She considered lying for half a second, purely on principle, and gave up on it. "No. We're going to the cafe."

"There it is." He looked pleased with himself in a way that made it hard to be annoyed at him for it.

She carried her plate to the sink, rinsed it, and grabbed her keys off the hook by the door. "Milk, eggs, bread, croissant," she said, ticking them off. "Anything else, or is that the full order?"

"That's the full order. You're a great assistant."

"I'm not your assistant, I'm doing you a favor."

"Same thing, different tone."

She rolled her eyes at him, but there wasn't much behind it. "See you later."

"Have fun. Watch out for tables."

"No promises there either."

She stepped out and pulled the door shut behind her, the latch catching with its familiar small click, the morning already warm enough on the porch that she could feel it settle onto her shoulders before she'd even made it to the steps.
