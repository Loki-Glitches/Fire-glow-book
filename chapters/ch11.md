# Chapter 11 — Elizabeth

He came by again the next evening, and the one after that, until the visits stopped needing an excuse and started just happening — the way Sophie's did, or the mailman's, except the mailman didn't sit on her porch step until the streetlights came on.

Tonight he'd shown up before she'd even finished the dishes, and she'd waved him up onto the step with a soapy hand through the screen door rather than making him wait. Sam was somewhere in the back of the house with the television low. The porch light was still dead. The bulb was still in its sleeve by her foot, and she still hadn't asked anyone to put it in.

"You're early," she said, drying her hands on the dish towel she'd carried out with her.

"Am I."

"You usually wait until it's actually dark." She sat on the top step and left him the spot below it, which had become his without either of them deciding that on purpose. "Guess you ran out of things to do."

"Something like that."

She watched him settle into the step the way he always did — like a man choosing a position and then not moving from it for an hour. He'd started asking her things lately that weren't quite the same shape as before. Not what she liked, not what made her laugh, not the easy inventory of a person getting to know another person. Lately it was smaller and stranger — how long she'd had the dish towel, whether she'd always hung it over her shoulder like that, why she parked the way she did in a driveway with room to spare. Little things. Things that didn't add up to anything she could point at, except that they were starting to add up to something.

"You've been doing this a lot," she said.

He looked over. "Doing what."

"Digging." She turned the towel over once in her hands, working out how to put it. "Not the normal way. Like — you already know what kind of person I am. You've got that part down. Now it's like you're after everything else. The specifics. The stuff nobody actually asks about."

He didn't answer right away, which by now she knew wasn't the same as not having an answer.

"What did you notice, exactly," he said. "About it."

She glanced at him — that was new too. He didn't usually ask her to say more once she'd already said something. He usually let it sit there and moved past it, the way she did with the things he didn't answer.

"I don't know. Just that it changed." She shrugged, and it came out lighter than the shrug felt. "I noticed you early on. At the door, actually — Tristan's the one who does the introductions, and you just kind of stood there taking everything in, like you were cataloguing the porch furniture. I thought that was strange. Then I thought it was interesting. I didn't think much past interesting." She left it there. She wasn't going to hand him the rest of that thought free of charge.

He accepted that she'd stopped, which she appreciated more than she said.

"Okay, my turn," she said, and shifted so she was facing him properly. "You get to go through my whole life rent-free. I get one back."

"That's not how this works."

"It's exactly how this works. I decided." She tucked the towel over the porch rail. "You're an angel. You've told me that much and basically nothing else. So — what can you actually do?"

Something in his face didn't change, which was itself an answer of a kind. "I don't have any real power."

"That's not an answer, that's a deflection with better posture."

"It's the truth." He said it the way he said most things — flat, unbothered, entirely without the performance of trying to convince her. "Whatever you're picturing, I don't have it. I haven't had it since before you were born."

She studied him for a second, trying to find the seam where the lie would be, and couldn't find one. That was almost more unsettling than if he'd been obviously hiding something. "Then what can you do?"

"Nothing worth showing you."

"Then what CAN you do." She wasn't going to let this go, and he seemed to understand that faster than most people did — most people got two tries out of her before she moved on. He was already looking at the porch like it had personally inconvenienced him.

He was quiet for a moment. Not thinking, exactly — more like deciding which was less trouble, arguing with her or just getting it over with.

"Fine," he said, and stood.

There was a planter at the corner of the porch that Sam had been meaning to move for two summers — poured concrete, chipped at one lip, the kind of thing that took two people and a dolly and usually didn't get moved at all because of it. Adrian crossed to it and stood over it for a moment before doing anything. It wasn't a long moment. It was just a fraction longer than the task should have needed — the kind of pause a person takes before stepping onto ice they haven't tested, except his face gave away nothing about it being ice at all.

Then he crouched, got his hands under the lip, and lifted it clean off the porch boards like it was an empty flowerpot. He set it down a few feet over, out of the walking path, straightened, and looked at her like the conversation had already moved on.

Elizabeth hadn't moved. "Okay. Okay — that's not nothing."

"It's not much."

"You just picked up four hundred pounds of concrete like it was a shoebox and you're telling me it's not much."

"It's not much," he said again, and sat back down on his step, already done with it in a way that made it clear he wasn't going to explain it further even if she asked. She did ask, twice, and got a shrug both times, and eventually let it go the way she let most things go with him — filed, not forgotten.

The light had shifted while they'd been talking, the flat gold of early evening tipping over into something deeper at the edges. She noticed it the way she noticed most sunsets, which was late and all at once.

"Oh — look at that." She leaned to see past the porch overhang, which cut the sky off right where it was getting good. "You can never see it properly from down here. The roof gets the whole thing."

"You've been up there."

"Once. When I was twelve, and Sam nearly had a heart attack about it." She was still leaning, chasing a better angle that didn't exist from the step. "I've wanted to go back up ever since and I've never had a good enough reason."

He looked at the porch post, then at her, and something settled into a decision behind his face that she couldn't read and didn't try to.

"Come here," he said.

She got up without asking why, which surprised her a little even as she did it. He braced one hand against the post and laced his fingers together low, the way you'd offer a leg-up over a fence, except when she stepped into it he lifted her straight to the roof's edge like she weighed nothing at all, easy enough that she barely had to reach for the gutter before she was already sitting on the shingles.

"Show-off," she said, twisting around to look down at him.

"You asked."

He didn't bother with the post. He got one hand on the edge of the roofline and came up after her in a single motion, unhurried, like he was stepping over a low fence rather than climbing the side of a house. He settled onto the shingles beside her without so much as breathing hard.

"That's cheating."

"You didn't specify rules."

The shingles still held the day's heat, warm enough through her jeans that she didn't mind sitting still on them. From up here the porch roof didn't block anything — the whole sky was laid open above the yards and the fence lines, orange bleeding into something closer to red where it met the tree line, the color deepening while she watched it.

She glanced over to see if he was as unimpressed by it as he usually seemed to be by anything ordinary, and he wasn't. He was watching the horizon with an attention she hadn't seen him give to anything since she'd met him — not scanning it, not cataloguing it, just looking, like the sky had asked something of him and he was finally answering.

She laughed before she meant to — a short, surprised sound. "You've genuinely never seen a sunset before, have you."

"I've seen plenty of sunsets."

"Not like that you haven't." She pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them, watching him instead of the sky now, because this was better. "You look like it personally offended you by being that pretty."

He didn't answer that one, which she took as its own kind of admission.

They sat like that until the color had gone all the way to the deep end of the spectrum, until the first stars had started to show through where the orange had thinned. He leaned back at some point without her quite noticing when, bracing on his palms against the shingles, and then all the way back, until he was lying flat against the slope of the roof with his face turned up at the sky. His eyes closed.

She thought at first he might be listening for something — some old habit of watching for a threat even with his eyes shut — but his shoulders had gone loose against the shingles in a way she hadn't seen from him before, not once, not even in the quietest moments on the porch. He wasn't waiting for anything. He wasn't holding anything in place. He was just there, flat on a roof, doing nothing at all.

A smile came up at the corner of his mouth. Small. Unguarded. Nothing behind it that she could name, and nothing performing itself for her benefit — he didn't even know she was looking.

She sat with that for a second longer than she needed to, then let it go and looked back out at what was left of the sunset, because some things didn't need to be understood right away to be kept.

"You're going to fall asleep up here," she said eventually.

"I don't sleep."

"Could've fooled me."

He opened his eyes again, unbothered, and pushed himself back up onto his elbows like he hadn't just given her the most honest ten minutes she'd ever gotten out of him.

The sky had gone mostly to navy by the time they climbed back down — him first this time, dropping to the porch boards without any visible effort, then reaching up to catch her at the waist and set her down beside him before she'd even finished swinging her legs over the edge.

"I could've managed the ladder myself," she said, straightening her shirt.

"There isn't a ladder."

"That was a general statement about my competence."

He almost smiled again — she caught the start of it before he put it away, which felt like watching someone close a door gently instead of slamming it. They stood there a moment on the dark porch, close enough that she could have said something about the day, or the roof, or any of the ordinary things people said to fill the last minute before a goodbye, and she didn't reach for any of them. Neither did he.

"I should go," he said, and it came out slower than it usually did, like the sentence cost him something small to finish.

"Okay."

He went down the steps, past the planter still sitting where he'd left it, out toward the gate. She stayed on the porch and watched him the whole way, the way she always did, except tonight neither of them filled the space with anything — no goodnight, no see-you-tomorrow, nothing needing to be said to mean it. He glanced back once before the gate, just once, and she was still there, and that was the whole exchange.

She stood there a while after he'd gone, arms crossed against the cooling air, and didn't try to name whatever had just happened. She just knew that whatever it was, she'd liked it, and that she'd have gone back up on that roof every night for a month if it meant seeing him look like that again.
