# My Usual — Elizabeth

The automatic doors at Higgins' stuck a little on the way in and Elizabeth gave the left one the small shoulder bump it needed before it finally slid the rest of the way open. The blast of air conditioning after the parking lot heat was enough to make her eyes water for a second. She grabbed a cart from the row by the entrance, checked that its wheels weren't the kind that pulled hard to one side, and pushed off toward produce.

The list in her head wasn't really a list. It was more of a shape: milk, eggs, bread that wasn't full of seeds Sam would pick out one by one and leave in a little pile on his plate, and then whatever else looked like it belonged in a kitchen that two people actually used. She dropped a bag of apples into the cart out of habit and then stood there a second looking at it, not sure why she'd grabbed apples specifically when neither of them ate that many, before shrugging and moving on.

"Elizabeth! Morning, sweetheart."

Mrs. Alvarez was restocking peaches two bins down, sleeves pushed to her elbows, a produce apron tied twice around her waist because it was sized for someone bigger.

"Morning, Mrs. Alvarez. Those peaches any good this week?"

"Better than good."

She picked out four, more out of politeness than need, and kept moving. In the bread aisle a teenage boy in a store polo was restacking a display that had half collapsed, and she recognized the back of his head before he turned around.

"Hey, Toby."

"Oh — hey, Liz." He straightened up, an armful of rolls threatening to spill. "You need help finding anything?"

"I think I know my way around by now."

He laughed like she'd said something funnier than she had and went back to the rolls. She found the loaf Sam liked, the dense kind with nothing hiding in it, and set it in the cart next to the peaches.

By the dairy case she ran into Mr. Higgins himself, apron a darker green than his employees', he pretended to be checking expiration dates on the yogurt but was really just watching the store.

"Elizabeth. Tell your brother his invoice from last month cleared, no rush on the next one."

"He'll be glad to hear it."

"Good kid, that one. Both of you." He said, and went back to the yogurt.

She filled the cart the rest of the way without much more conversation — eggs, milk, a second gallon because Sam went through it faster than made sense for one person, a bag of coffee for the machine at home even though she'd end up buying coffee somewhere else today anyway, some vegetables she had reasonable intentions about cooking. It didn't take long.

The checkout lanes were mostly empty for a Tuesday morning, and she picked the one with the shortest line, which was also the one nearest the front windows. While the cashier — a woman she didn't know by name, new enough that she still wore her badge turned the wrong way out — scanned everything through, Elizabeth found herself looking past her, out through the glass toward the parking lot.

Two men she didn't recognize were walking near the edge of the lot, by the low brick wall that separated the store's parking from the sidewalk. One of them wore dark clothes, all of it too heavy for the weather — long sleeves, dark trousers, nothing that made sense for late summer — and moved with his weight shifted onto one leg, arms crossed, looking off down the street. The other, beside him, dressed more like he belonged somewhere — lighter shirt, sleeves rolled, hands in his pockets — was doing most of the talking, or at least his head kept turning toward the first man while the first man mostly didn't turn back.

She didn't know either of them. That wasn't unusual on its own — people passed through, took the highway exit into town for gas and a meal and left again — but something about the two of them made her watch a beat longer than she meant to.

"That'll be sixty-one forty," the cashier said, and Elizabeth blinked back to her own register and handed over the card.

She bagged everything herself while she waited for the receipt, glancing out the window again without quite meaning to. The two men hadn't moved much. The one in the heavy dark clothes had turned his head now, looking up the street toward the hill at the edge of town, and the other had said something that made him look briefly annoyed all over again.

She took her bags, thanked the cashier, and pushed the cart out through the doors, still glancing in that direction. When a hand landed on her shoulder from behind and she nearly came out of her skin.

"Soph—" She spun, one hand pressed flat to her own chest. "You scared the life out of me."

Sophie was laughing before she'd even finished being sorry about it, hands up in mock surrender, sunglasses pushed up into her hair, a loose tank top and shorts that said she hadn't planned on doing anything more strenuous today than what they were currently doing. "I said your name twice. You were somewhere else entirely."

"I was picking out peaches in my head, apparently, even after I already bought them."

"Sure you were." Sophie peered past her shoulder toward the parking lot, curious, but whatever she was looking for wasn't there anymore, and she let it go. "Come on, pop the trunk situation, I'm parked right over here."

Sophie's car was two rows over, and between the two of them the bags went in fast — Sophie taking the heavier ones without being asked and stacking them so nothing would tip. When the cart was empty Elizabeth wheeled it back across the lot to the corral by the entrance and gave it a shove in with the others, and by the time she got back Sophie already had the engine running and the radio down low.

"Okay," Sophie said, pulling out of the lot, "tell me everything, I haven't seen you in like four days, which for us is basically a geological era."

"There's not much. Sam made breakfast. I lost a fight this morning and got a cut on my head."

"You what?"

"It's fine, it barely hurt this morning. I just clipped the nightstand." She touched the spot above her eyebrow without meaning to, found it hadn't scabbed over as much as she expected by now, and put her hand back in her lap. "Also there was another postcard. London this time."

"From your mom?"

"Her handwriting. 'Greetings from London, kids.' That's it. That's the whole thing."

Sophie glanced over at a red light, reading something off her face that Elizabeth hadn't said out loud. "You okay?"

"Yeah… Mostly."

Sophie didn't push. She reached over and squeezed Elizabeth's knee once, quick, and then the light turned green and she was talking about something else — a fight she and Sam had almost had over whose turn it was to pick the movie, resolved by the fact that neither of them had actually wanted to watch anything and they'd ended up just talking until midnight instead.

The coffee house sat two blocks off Main, in a converted storefront with the original tile floor still intact and a chalkboard menu that hadn't changed in years except for the seasonal drink written in different colors each time. Sophie held the door for her, the bell over it announcing them, and got in line behind a man ordering four drinks at once for what was apparently an entire office.

"I'm getting the cookie," Sophie said, studying the pastry case like it might surprise her. "The big one, not the little one. And my usual, obviously."

"Obviously." Elizabeth teased.

When it was Elizabeth's turn she ordered her own iced coffee first — for Sam, who'd have opinions about it if it wasn't exactly how he liked it, so she specified it twice to be sure — and then a chocolate croissant to go with it, and then, for herself, the fruity iced drink she got almost every time she came here and a plain croissant to go with it.

"That's a lot of pastry for two people," the girl behind the counter said, ringing it up.

"One of them's not for me."

"Sure," the girl said and Elizabeth decided everyone in this town found her mildly amusing today.

They took the corner table by the window once their names got called, the one with the wobbly leg that Sophie always insisted on anyway out of some sense of loyalty to it. Sophie broke her cookie in half without asking and pushed one piece across the table, which was its own kind of tax Elizabeth had stopped fighting a long time ago.

"So," Sophie said, once she'd gotten a decent start on her iced coffee, "how nervous are you actually. About the orientation thing. Real answer, not the Sam answer."

"There's a difference?"

"You know there's a difference."

Elizabeth gave her cup a quarter turn on the table, watching the ice settle. "A little nervous. Not about the classes part. About — I don't know. Leaving him alone in that house for the first time since forever."

"He's not going to fall apart, Liz."

"I know that."

"But you're going to worry about it anyway."

"Probably the whole first semester." She said it lightly enough that it came out sounding like a joke, though it wasn't entirely one, but Sophie let it be whatever size Elizabeth wanted it to be, which was most of what made her easy to talk to.

"For what it's worth," Sophie said, "I'll be checking on him. Regularly. Possibly overbearingly."

"I know you will. That's the only reason I'm not more nervous."

They stayed longer than either of them had probably planned.The conversation drifted from college to a girl they'd both gone to school with who was apparently engaged now to someone twice her age, to whether Sophie should cut her hours at the salon before the fall or wait, to nothing in particular at all. Elizabeth ate her croissant in pieces, pulling the corners off first, and somewhere in the middle of it mentioned, almost as an afterthought, that she'd seen a couple of guys she didn't recognize outside Higgins', standing around not doing much of anything.

"Probably just passing through," Sophie said, already onto the next topic before Elizabeth had fully finished the sentence.

"Yeah. I don't know, I guess."

When they finally stood to leave, the pastry bag with Sam's croissant in it swinging from Elizabeth's wrist and his iced coffee sweating through a paper sleeve in her other hand, Sophie pushed through the door first, still talking over her shoulder about something Elizabeth wasn't quite listening to anymore.

Sophie walked straight into someone coming the other way, hard enough that her iced coffee left her hand entirely.
