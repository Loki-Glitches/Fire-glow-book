# Interrogations — Elizabeth

Elizabeth typed the text three times before she actually sent it.

*Hey — any chance you and Adrian are free today? Want to check the park, see if anyone's spotted her.*

Delete. Too chipper, like she was inviting them to brunch.

*Could use your help looking for Eve today, meet at the park?*

Delete. Too much like a business memo.

*Meet me at the park benches this morning. We have work to do.*

Sophie would've handled this normally. Sophie had a shift at the salon, though, which meant the job of personally reaching out to two men Elizabeth actively disliked fell squarely in her own lap. Fantastic. She didn't examine that math too hard. She wanted Eve found, somebody had to actually do something useful about it today.

Tristan showed up at the park gate first, hands in his pockets, looking almost like a normal person for once. Adrian trailed a step closely behind him.

Elizabeth didn't bother hiding the eye-roll. "Of course."

"What?" Tristan asked. "I figured you wanted to talk to both of us."

"Fine. Whatever. Let's just go." She stood up and led the way to the surrounding stores.

Her reasoning, such as it was: the park sat dead center of the town, storefronts surrounding it on every side, which made it the single best place to ask a lot of people the same question fast. If Eve had so much as breathed near this town, somebody here would've noticed it.

Nobody had.

Mrs. Okafor at the bakery barely looked up from wiping flour off her hands. "Dark hair, foreign-sounding? No, sweetheart. Try Hank at the hardware store — man sees everybody eventually, whether they want to be seen or not."

Hank had seen nobody, but he had thoughts regardless. "Woman wandering around, nobody knowing her, somebody'd have called the sheriff's office by now. Small town. We all notice things eventually." He gave Tristan and Adrian a once-over while he said it, the exact look of a man doing quiet speculations on two strangers and not yet sure whether to contact the sheriff himself.

The pharmacist, a guy named Collins who looked like he hadn't slept since the Clinton administration, didn't even bother with a follow-up question. "Nope. Sorry. Good luck."

Three for three, and Elizabeth was starting to feel like a door-to-door vacuum salesman with nothing to sell.

One stop broke rhythm. The flower shop on the corner had pots spilling off the sidewalk in every direction, and the woman running it introduced herself as Ruth, like names were the first order of business and Eve was a distant second.

"Haven't seen her," Ruth said, after actually thinking about it instead of reflexively saying no. "You tried the church bulletin board? Half this town finds out everything from that corkboard before they find out from each other. Faster than the news, slower than the diner." She looked between the three of them, unbothered, entirely at her own pace. "She got family looking too, or is it just you three?"

"Just us," Elizabeth said.

"Mm." Ruth's attention drifted, settled on Adrian, and stayed there a beat longer than strictly necessary. "You two new in town?"

"Something like that."

"Didn't think I recognized you." She considered him like she would size up a stray that kept showing up on your porch — not suspicious, just curious whether it was worth feeding. "You look like you could use a plant that isn't actively trying to die on you. Half my stock is nearly impossible to kill. Come on back if you want something that survives neglect."

"I'll keep that in mind."

"You say that to be polite. I mean it, though." She waved them off down the block and went back to her watering can, entirely unbothered either way.

It stuck with Elizabeth longer than a plant recommendation had any right to. Nobody else in this entire town had managed to just be nice to him, no agenda riding shotgun underneath it. She didn't do anything with the observation. She just kept it in her back pocket and kept walking, feeling faintly, uselessly sorry for a man she was still mad at.

By the time they'd hit every storefront on the square, they had nothing. A whole lot of nothing, several ways nothing could be phrased.

"She's not here," Elizabeth said, dropping onto a bench by the fountain. "We've been at this for an hour and nobody's seen so much as a ghost of her."

"My thoughts exactly," Tristan said, taking the bench across from her, Adrian settling beside him. "We've been here for almost a week."

"Maybe she already left," Elizabeth offered, then realized that neither of them was listening.

"I watched her leave," Adrian said. Flat. Apparently that was the whole contribution.

Tristan picked up the slack, which Elizabeth was starting to suspect was just his job. "That doesn't tell us when she lands, or if she has yet. We don't know her timing. We know she's coming, and we're fairly sure it's here specifically."

"That's not an answer, that's a vibe," Elizabeth argued.

"It's the only one we've got," Tristan countered.

"Okay, but what does that actually mean? Logistically. Do we just loiter around a park for the foreseeable future?" Elizabeth questioned.

"Yes. We wait in town," Tristan restated. "We keep doing exactly what you've been doing." He looked at Elizabeth.

Adrian offered nothing. He was looking at the fountain blankly, apparently having used up his one line for the hour.

"Great. Riveting plan, guys. Really inspired strategy session."

"It's a start," Tristan said, entirely unbothered by the sarcasm, which took some of the fun out of it.

At least Tristan gave her something to work with though. Adrian just sat there radiating the general energy of a man waiting for a very late bus.

Eventually Tristan stood, and Adrian followed half a beat later, already halfway into a goodbye nobody had approved.

"Sit. Down." Elizabeth snapped, like she was training a dog. "Both of you."

Tristan sat out of pure surprise. Adrian took his time about it, clearly deciding whether her tone had earned his cooperation.

"I have questions," Elizabeth said. "Real ones this time. Like the ones Sophie tried to get out of you over text and got next to nothing for her trouble."

"That," Adrian said, before she'd even gotten to one, "is a separate problem. He shouldn't have answered anything over text in the first place, especially the questions about me." Not looking at her. Looking at his brother.

"I answered what she asked." Tristan shrugged.

"I know." Adrian rolled his eyes, looking annoyed by his brother's response.

"You could just not let it bother you." Elizabeth crossed her arms and legs.

Adrian didn't dignify that with a response. His hand went to his jacket pocket instead and came out holding yet another apple Elizabeth was fairly sure hadn't been there two seconds ago. He split it clean in half with his bare hands — no knife, no visible strain, like tearing a piece of paper — and held one half out to his brother without a word, the universal gesture for we are done discussing this.

Tristan took it and ate it without comment, which Elizabeth read as surrender dressed up as nonchalance.

"Fine," she said. "Then answer me directly Adrian. Why don't you have a phone?"

She'd braced for a shrug, or nothing, or some repeat of the I don't like them she'd already gotten secondhand. What actually came back had some weight on it.

"I've watched a lot of people waste away staring at those things," Adrian said, around his first bite. "I simply don't see the benefits of the modern age. They have become such grand sources of distractions."

Not an answer built for follow-up questions. Her jaw dropped given that it was the most honest thing he'd said to her all day and closed it quickly to not be awkward.

They kept eating as the conversation rolled along, unhurried, a bite here and there between sentences, and somewhere in there Elizabeth noticed something shifting in the men that she couldn't immediately name. Tristan had gone looser — easier with his own words, less like a man reciting a report. Adrian, against all odds, was talking. Actually talking, more words volunteered in the last five minutes than she'd gotten out of him across their entire acquaintance combined.

She caught herself staring, trying to do the math on what exactly had changed.

"What," Adrian said, catching her at it.

"Nothing." She shook it off — whatever it was, it didn't add up to anything she could actually point at — and went for the real question that had been with her since she met him.

Now that she was paying attention, she realized he'd been doing it this entire conversation — looking at her forehead, her hairline, somewhere comfortably north of her actual eyes, every single time.

"Why don't you ever look at me when we talk?" Elizabeth raised an eyebrow to elicit a straight answer.

He didn't answer right away. And Elizabeth opened her mouth to ask a different question.

"I hadn't noticed I was doing that. I apologize," He said it like the fact had just been handed to him fresh, like she'd opened a drawer he didn't know he had.

Tristan stopped eating mid-bite and turned a wide-eyed face to look at Adrian.

"Huh." She gave him a second to add something. He genuinely hadn't known about it, and there was no version of her pushing further that wasn't overly prying with extra steps.

"Last one," she said. "What do you actually do for fun?"

"Art." Out fast, too fast, clearly meant as a conversation-ender rather than an opener. Then, like he'd heard the word land and didn't love it: "But I haven't done it lately. Work's been — a lot and I lost some of my passion for it."

"What kind of art?" Elizabeth pressed gently.

Adrian sighed like he was rethinking the answer he gave but he didn't stop. "I do all of the different arts, but like I said, I haven't done it for a while."

"Cool. I guess it's my turn to apologize for pressing when you clearly didn't want to answer."

"Can we go now?" he asked gently, already half standing, like the sentence and the motion were the same gesture.

"Yeah," she said it just as softly.

The men left without any further ceremony. Elizabeth watched them cross the square, then let her head drop back against the top of the bench, staring at absolutely nothing.

"Finally," she said, to an audience of no one.
