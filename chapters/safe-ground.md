Morning light came through the windows of the mansion's front room and spread across a floor of deep red and gold. Lucifer had redone the space from nothing: a pair of wingback armchairs in oxblood velvet, facing each other across a rug woven in garnet and black; a mahogany sideboard carved along its edges with climbing vines, its surface holding a crystal decanter and two matching glasses; a gilt-framed mirror hung above a swept, cold fireplace flanked by a pair of bronze candlesticks taller than his forearm. Heavy damask curtains the color of dried wine pooled a foot onto the floor at each window, and a chandelier hung low over the center of the room, its crystal drops catching the light and scattering it in small, moving pieces across the ceiling.

He was in the larger of the two armchairs, upholstered in the same oxblood as the other, when Azrael came down the stairs and stopped in the doorway to take the room in.

"You redid the whole thing." Azrael didn't sound surprised so much as resigned to it.

"I redid one room. It needed it — I wanted a room in this house that doesn't remind anyone it used to be on fire."

Azrael walked a slow circuit of the room, trailing two fingers along the sideboard's carved edge, pausing at the cold fireplace, tipping his head to study a framed landscape painting hung between the windows that he didn't remember from before. "Go big or go home."

"Something like that."

Azrael looked up at the chandelier a moment longer, its crystal drops still faintly swaying, then let out a breath through his nose and dropped the subject, same as always — not resignation exactly, just the long practice of knowing which things were worth saying more about. He crossed to the window instead and stood there with his back to the light.

Lucifer waved him toward the other armchair. "Sit down. I'm not craning my neck at you all morning."

Azrael took the other armchair, dropping into it with none of the care Lucifer had put into choosing the fabric. For a while neither of them said anything. It was, Lucifer thought, close enough to comfortable — the kind of quiet two people could still sit through without it turning into anything, after six thousand years of practice at exactly that.

Lucifer let the quiet run a while longer before he broke it. "One of the rooms upstairs. I was thinking — make it a studio."

Azrael turned his head. "A studio for what."

"I haven't decided yet. Something of my own, is all. A room in this house that isn't about either of us staying, or finding her, or any of the rest of it."

Azrael held his gaze a moment, then nodded once and let it go without pushing.

He let the idea sit there after that, unargued either way — he still hadn't decided whether to leave the upstairs plaster as it was or do something about it, and for once he didn't mind not deciding. It was only talk. Nothing about it needed settling this morning, or any morning soon.

Azrael let it go too, same as he had the room, and moved on to the thing he'd actually come downstairs to say. "We should go into town today."

"We just came from there."

Azrael said it flat and unbothered, arms crossed, more like a man reading off a list than making a case. "We should go again. Watch the square, see if she's shown herself. And I want a better read on this place before she does — who's who, what passes for strange here, what doesn't. It's recon. Nothing more."

"I don't want to go." He said it plainly, no heat in it yet, just a fact he expected to be respected. "I'd rather stay here."

"I didn't ask what you wanted."

"You're asking me to stand around a town I've already stood around in, looking for a woman who isn't here, so you can learn the names of people who don't matter."

"That's the job."

Lucifer didn't answer that right away. He didn't actually disagree — it was the job, and he knew it — he just didn't want to be the one standing there for it today.

They went back and forth on it a few more rounds, Lucifer's objections getting shorter and more irritated as Azrael's stayed exactly as even as they'd started — which was its own kind of provocation, the fact that nothing Lucifer said ever seemed to cost his brother anything. Eventually Azrael simply stood up.

"I'm going. You can come or not."

He walked out of the room without waiting to see which Lucifer chose, and Lucifer felt it the moment Azrael crossed the threshold — the tether pulling taut somewhere under his ribs, patient and certain, the kind of pull that didn't argue back. He knew exactly what his brother was doing. Azrael hadn't raised his voice once, hadn't so much as looked annoyed; he'd simply walked toward the door, confident that the house would do the rest of the convincing for him.

Lucifer swore under his breath and got up before the pull had any real chance to sharpen, catching up to Azrael on the gravel drive rather than arriving at his side in a heap on it. He'd rather walk there under his own power than be hauled there, even if the destination came out the same either way.

"You could have just asked," he said, falling in beside him on the gravel.

Azrael didn't bother answering that. He didn't need to.

They went down the hill together, the town laid out below them in the ordinary morning business of itself — trucks idling outside the hardware store, someone sweeping a storefront, the plaza's fountain still dry in its ring of benches. Lucifer walked it already irritated, and in that mood every detail read as an imposition, a thing the day was doing to him rather than something simply happening.

Higgins' was busy enough that they had to wait behind two carts to get through the door, and it was just inside it, by the second register, that Lucifer heard his own name — the human one — called out across the store.

"Adrian! Tristan!"

James had Claire's hand in his, both of them wearing the particular glow of people who had recently decided something large about their lives and hadn't yet gotten tired of saying so.

"We were just talking about you two," Claire said, steering them both out of the checkout line and toward the end of an aisle, where she could talk without blocking traffic. "Well — not you specifically. Weddings. We're doing some early planning."

James rocked back on his heels, clearly proud of having thought it through already. "Nothing real yet. Just talking through where. We want it here. In town."

Azrael inclined his head. "That's good. Towns like this one don't mind an excuse for a party."

"That's what I said." James grinned at Claire, who rolled her eyes fondly and let him have it.

Claire glanced between the two brothers, checking whether the answer had changed since the orchard. "Between places still?"

Azrael didn't blink at the repeat question. "Still between places. We found somewhere to live. It's a bit run down, but it'll work for now."

He said it with a flatness that closed the subject before it had properly opened — not rude, just final, the kind of sentence that gave a person nothing to ask a follow-up question about. Claire took it exactly that way and didn't push.

James cut in before the subject could drift further. "Any luck finding her? The woman you were looking for?"

Azrael shook his head. "Not yet. Long, wavy black hair, if either of you sees her. She might not land on the right language with you, either — don't be surprised if she tries something other than English first."

Claire glanced at James, then back. "We'll keep an eye out. Haven't seen anyone like that, though. Not here, not at the bakery, nowhere."

Azrael nodded once. "Let us know if you do."

Claire nodded, already moving past it — there was nothing in that answer worth holding onto, and she didn't try. "How's it going with you and Liz, by the way?" she asked, lighter now, the kind of question asked for the pleasure of asking it rather than because the answer mattered much. "James saw her outside Higgins' the other week and said you two looked like you were about to kill each other."

Azrael cut in before anyone else could, even and unhurried as ever. "We're working it out. She doesn't love Adrian much yet."

He added it with a small shrug, nothing in the gesture meant to soften the point. "It's fair, of course."

Lucifer said nothing. He had nothing to say to that and no particular wish to; he stood where he was and let his brother do the talking, same as he had all morning, and found he minded being discussed more than he minded anything actually said about him.

There was nothing in this aisle he wanted to look at — rows of boxed cereal, a cardboard display of charcoal bags, a hand-lettered sign over the registers advertising a sale on something he didn't care about. He let his eyes move over all of it anyway, because it gave him somewhere to put his attention that wasn't the conversation. Claire was asking Azrael something about venues now — whether the plaza itself could be rented for a day, whether anyone did that here — and Azrael was answering with the same even patience he answered everything, and none of it required Lucifer to be present for it in any way beyond standing where he was.

He counted the number of register lanes. He counted them again. Somewhere behind him a child kept asking a parent for something, loud and insistent about it, and he found himself listening to that instead, simply because it changed more often than the wedding talk did.

"We really should get back to it," James said eventually, tugging Claire gently by the hand she still hadn't let go of. "We've got a call with her mom in twenty minutes about flowers, of all things."

Azrael lifted a hand in farewell. "Good luck."

"You too. With her." James tipped his chin at Lucifer, friendly, seemingly unaware of how strange the whole exchange about Liz had just sounded with her standing nowhere near them. "She'll come around."

They said their goodbyes and went off toward the front of the store, already talking over each other about flowers.

Lucifer exhaled once they were out of earshot. "That took a while."

Azrael didn't dispute it. They left without buying anything, the errand that had brought them down the hill in the first place having evidently been satisfied by standing in an aisle listening to other people's plans, and started back up toward the house.

---

By the time they reached the drive, Lucifer had decided he wasn't going to touch the house today. There was a window on the second floor still missing its glass and a section of banister that wanted resetting, and he looked at both of them from the front hall and felt nothing that resembled the will to fix either.

"No work today."

Azrael, who'd come to expect the one-repair rule to hold every day without exception, raised an eyebrow at that but didn't object.

Lucifer stood in the middle of the front hall instead, under a ceiling that was plaster in some places and open sky in others, a web of old scorch marks still crawling up one wall from the fire that had gutted the place decades before he'd ever bought it. He spread one hand upward, speaking out loud, same as always, with no particular faith it would be heard.

"I want this house to hold me. Not the way a wall holds anything up. The way an anchor does. Somewhere I don't need him within twenty feet of me to stay standing in — and somewhere he can walk off from without dragging me after him like a dog on a line." He paused. "If you're listening. Which you usually aren't."

Nothing answered him. No voice, no weight in the air, no sign that the request had reached anyone at all. He hadn't expected one — he never did — but he stood there a moment longer anyway, giving the silence one more chance before he let it go.

Azrael considered the empty ceiling a moment. "Well. No harm in finding out."

"None at all."

"I'll walk it off, then."

Lucifer stayed where he was, in the front hall, on the scorched floorboards he hadn't gotten around to fixing, and watched his brother go — out the front door, down the drive, past the gatepost, out well beyond the distance either of them had ever needed to measure twice. He braced for it without meaning to, his whole body already drawing in against the pull, same as every time, a half-second ahead of it actually landing — the tether under his ribs going taut, the ground about to tilt out from under him.

It didn't come.

Azrael kept walking, further than twenty feet by a wide margin now, and nothing in Lucifer moved to follow him. He stood in the hall with the morning light coming in through the window at his back, still standing, still exactly where he'd chosen to be. Azrael turned around at the end of the drive and looked back at him, hands open at his sides, not bothering to ask the question out loud.

The house held still around him.

It had worked.
