# Chapter 9 — Elizabeth

Tristan was the one who moved first, stepping back from where he'd been standing near the end of the bench.

"I have somewhere to be."

Adrian looked up at him. Something passed between the two of them again, the same wordless current Elizabeth had been catching all afternoon and still couldn't get a hand on — there, then gone, whatever Adrian answered with living entirely in his face.

"That wasn't the arrangement," Adrian said.

"It is now." Tristan's voice had gone flat the same way it had at her front door that morning, brisk at the edges. "Get to know each other. It matters more right now than it sounds like it should."

"You don't trust me alone with her."

"I didn't say that." Tristan was already stepping back, hands returning to his pockets. "I have my own work. It doesn't wait for either of you."

He didn't tell Adrian to behave himself. He didn't look back once he'd said his piece, either — just crossed the grass toward the street at a pace that wasn't inviting company, and then he was gone, folded into the handful of people cutting through the park on their way somewhere else.

Elizabeth watched the space where he'd been for a second longer than she needed to. "Is he actually going somewhere, or is that also a version of an answer."

"He's going somewhere."

"You sound sure."

"He doesn't say things he doesn't mean." Adrian's eyes went back to the dry fountain. "He's not built for it."

Which left the two of them, and the same stretch of park that had been theirs for the better part of an hour now — the fountain still dry, the kids from earlier locked in some renegotiation over whose turn it was with the ball, a jogger cutting the long way around the path with a dog that clearly wanted to be doing anything else.

Elizabeth tucked one leg up under her on the bench and worked at a knot that had found its way into her shoelace sometime during the walk over. "Okay. If I've got an angel to myself for a while, I'm not spending it on the mission."

"What."

"I mean I have different questions now." She got the knot loose and let the shoe hang off her toes. "What did you like to do? Back before all this. When you were younger."

He didn't answer right away. It wasn't the pause she'd gotten used to from him — the one that came right before a non-answer arrived wearing the shape of a real one. This one just sat there a while before he used it.

"There wasn't a 'before this' the way you mean it. Not at first. I existed before there was anything to exist inside of — no time, no place, nothing to measure myself against." He said it plainly, no weight added anywhere. "Then He found me. Brought me along while He built the rest of it."

"Built everything," she repeated. "Like — everything."

"Everything." Something eased in his face. Not quite a smile. "I followed Him around while He worked, mostly getting in the way. I watched the first oceans arrive with no waves in them yet — somebody had to teach them that trick — and mountains that couldn't hold their own shape for the first few tries."

"That sounds like it should be terrifying. Watching the actual world get built."

"It wasn't. It was the opposite of that." He looked at her, and for once the look didn't seem to be evaluating anything. "I watched Him make my siblings — whole rooms of them arriving at once, most of them with opinions already formed before they'd finished existing. And the animals. He let me name a few of the early ones, before He decided that job needed a better system than me."

"You got fired from naming animals."

"Reassigned." A short breath went through him that might have been the start of a laugh, if he'd let it get that far. "There was a creature early on that was mostly wings and indignation. I don't think it survives anywhere anymore, in any form. I still think about it sometimes."

"You have a favorite dead creature."

"I have several."

She laughed at that, loud enough that the jogger glanced over on her way past. "What were you like? Back then. Actually like."

"Light," he said, without hesitating over the word the way she'd expected him to. "Nothing had cost me anything yet. There's a version of easy that only exists before you've lost your first thing, and I got to have all of it, for a while."

She let that sit for a second, watching the two kids finally reach some kind of truce over the ball. "Tell me more about the garden, then. The actual people in it. Adam and Eve."

Something in him stopped, quiet and total. Nothing about him moved to announce it.

"Adam came after all that," Adrian said. "Long after. He was certain of things. Confident that whatever he didn't understand yet would either get explained to him or wouldn't matter." A beat. "It wasn't a compliment, in case that's how it landed."

"Noted." She smiled, small. "And Eve?"

He didn't answer that one the way he'd answered the others.

"She came later," he said, and stopped there.

He didn't add to it. His eyes had gone back to the fountain, and whatever had opened in his face a minute ago had folded itself shut again without any visible effort on his part.

Elizabeth waited another moment, mostly out of habit, then let it go. He hadn't owed her the whole garden just because he'd handed her a piece of it. She filed the shape of the gap away with everything else she was collecting today and looked back out at the kids instead.

The heat had thinned into the version that hummed instead of pressed, the late-day kind that meant the worst of it was behind them. She checked the time on her phone — later than she'd meant to let it get — and swung her leg down, shoe righted.

"I should get back. Sam gets weird if people miss dinner after he's spent all day threatening to make one." She stood, testing the shoe against the ground. "Are you walking me, or is that not part of the assignment?"

"It's part of it."

"Convenient."

He didn't argue that either.

They didn't talk much on the way. The quiet wasn't awkward — she'd spent her questions for the day, and he didn't fill silence for its own sake, and somewhere between the park and her street those two facts turned out to fit together fine. A couple of teenagers on bikes slowed to look at him the way most of the town seemed to on sight, decided against saying anything, and pedaled on past.

By the time they turned onto her street the sun had dropped low enough to throw everything sideways — long shadows off the mailboxes, off the fence posts, off the two of them walking between. Her porch came into view with the light above the door still dark, the same as it had been for what felt like a week now. The new bulb was probably still sitting where she'd left it on the step, in its plastic sleeve, exactly as useless there as it had been in the store.

The front door opened before she'd gotten her key anywhere near the lock — Sam, dish towel over one shoulder, already mid-sentence about timers.

"—which means if you're not sitting down in the next ten minutes, that's on you." He stopped when he registered someone standing behind her.

"This is Adrian," Elizabeth said, stepping past him into the doorway to give him a clear look. "Adrian — my brother, Sam."

Sam looked him over, unhurried about it, the towel still slung over his shoulder. "Nobody mentioned an Adrian."

"It's been a long day." Elizabeth toed off one shoe against the mat. "I'm mentioning him now."

"That's generous of you." Sam's eyes didn't move off him. "You got a last name, or are we not there yet?"

"We're not there yet," Adrian said.

"Fair enough." Sam held the door wider, though his attention stayed on him a beat longer than the gesture needed. "Dinner's in ten. You eating, or is this a drop-off?"

"I have somewhere to be." Adrian said it the same flat way he'd said it to Tristan an hour earlier.

"Course you do." Sam's mouth twitched. He didn't push it further — just held the door until Adrian had stepped back off the porch and onto the walk. "Nice meeting you."

"You too."

"So that's him," Sam said, low enough that it was mostly just for her, his eyes still tracking Adrian at the end of the walk.

"That's him."

"He give a straight answer to anything?"

"Not so far." Elizabeth toed off her other shoe and stepped past him into the house, the smell of dinner pulling her the rest of the way in. "Apparently it's a whole personality type."

Sam huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh and glanced down after her, one hand still on the door.

When he looked back up, the walk was empty.

He leaned out past the doorframe and checked both directions. Nothing — no one on the sidewalk, no car pulling away, no gate swinging shut on a neighbor's fence. Nothing to explain a person standing there one second and gone the next.

"Liz." Sam was still half outside, frowning at the empty street. "Where'd he go?"

She didn't answer. She was already three steps into the kitchen, out of earshot.

Sam stood in the doorway a moment longer, then shut the door on a street that had no explanation for him.

---

Lucifer had one foot already turned to leave when Elizabeth went up the last step to the porch. Sam's attention split between the two of them the way it had all evening — a glance for him, a longer look for her — right up until she crossed the threshold and his eyes dropped to follow her in.

That was the last full second of the evening Lucifer could account for.

The next one arrived on the ground.

There was no easing into it — just distance collapsing all at once, the street and the fence lines and the shape of the house going past him faster than a single moment could hold, and then pavement under his palms, one knee taking the rest of the impact.

He glared up at the sky before he'd even gotten his other hand under him. Once. He said nothing about it.

Azrael was already crossing the distance toward him by the time he got his feet back under him. "You weren't there. Then you were on the ground."

"I noticed."

Azrael studied him a moment. "That's not how it's supposed to work."

"Which part."

"Any of it."

"If you did something—" he started.

"I didn't." Azrael's voice had gone flat again, tight at the edges. "I've been standing on this corner for the better part of an hour. I didn't do anything."

Lucifer didn't press it further. There wasn't a version of the argument that went anywhere, so he let it drop instead of chasing it.

They walked back the way he'd apparently come — neither of them eager to test it twice by accident — until Elizabeth's street came into view, her porch light still dead above the door, no sign that anyone inside had noticed a thing.

"Stop there," Azrael said, a few houses short of hers. "Now walk toward it. Slowly."

Lucifer took one step, then a second, then—

He was gone. Simply not there, one foot still lifted mid-stride, the space where he'd been standing occupied by nothing at all.

Azrael was still staring at the empty sidewalk when something hit the pavement behind him. He turned to find Lucifer already pushing himself up off the ground, one palm scraped against the concrete.

"That's new," Azrael said.

Lucifer didn't have anything to say to that. "Go again," Azrael told him. "Further back this time."

He went further back. He vanished at almost the same spot, gone between one step and the next with no warning at all, and hit the ground beside Azrael half a second later, already midsentence in a thought he hadn't finished having yet.

They tried it from the mailbox at the end of the walk. Then from the fire hydrant two houses down, which put him further out but made no real difference — he came down at the same distance no matter which direction he approached from, dropping onto the pavement beside Azrael so many times, from a stranger's front lawn, from the middle of the street, from behind a parked car, that a dog two yards over quit barking at him and lay back down between rounds.

"Stand still," Azrael said, somewhere around the eighth or ninth try, more clipped than the last, one hand already out to keep from being knocked over again. "I'm trying to measure this."

"I'm not doing it on purpose."

"I didn't say you were." Azrael paced off the distance between the hydrant and the porch step, counting under his breath, then did it again when the first count didn't sit right with him. "Call it twenty feet."

"Give or take how much."

"I don't have a tape measure. I have you falling on me every time I ask you to take one more step." Azrael looked at the spot like it had personally inconvenienced him. "It's twenty feet. We're calling it twenty feet."

They tried it once more from the far side of the street, in case the sidewalk itself had anything to do with it. It didn't — he hit the ground at the same distance no matter which way he came at it, always beside Azrael, never closer, never farther. Twenty feet in a circle, not twenty feet in a line.

"You should've told me it did this," Lucifer said.

"I didn't know it did this." Azrael's voice had gone quiet in a way that wasn't quite an apology. "No one's ever taken one this far before."

Neither of them had an answer for that beyond the fact of it. Lucifer looked once more at the house, the dead porch light, the window with nothing moving behind it now.

"I need to be within that line by morning," he said. "Or near enough that it doesn't matter."

"Then be within it." Azrael was already turning to go, the conversation over as far as he was concerned. "Figure out the rest yourself."

He didn't look back to see whether Lucifer had heard him. He never did.
