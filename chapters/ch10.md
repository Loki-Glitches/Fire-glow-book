# Chapter 10 — Lucifer

In the days since the park, the arrangement had settled into something close to a schedule. He arrived in the evening, walked with her through whatever errand hadn't finished itself yet, and left before it turned into anything more than that. Tristan called it evaluation. Lucifer had stopped bothering to call it anything.

Tonight the door opened before he reached the top step, and Elizabeth didn't come out to meet him. She held it open instead.

"You're staying," she said. Not a question.

"Am I."

"Sam's made enough food for a family twice our size, and I'm not eating leftovers for four days because you decided you had somewhere else to be." She stepped back to clear the doorway. "So yes."

He weighed the sentence for anything that required arguing and found nothing. He went in.

The kitchen smelled like garlic bread and a sauce that had been simmering longer than it strictly needed. Sam stood at the stove with a wooden spoon in one hand and a towel over his shoulder. A pot rolled a slow boil beside a second one he hadn't touched yet. On the counter, a cutting board held the wreck of a loaf, sliced, buttered halfway, and abandoned mid-task.

"You again." Sam didn't look up from the stove.

"Apparently."

"He's staying this time," Elizabeth said, pulling two more stools out from under the counter. "I already decided. You don't have to like it."

"Didn't say I didn't like it." Sam turned the burner down and looked over — the same unhurried pass he'd given Lucifer on the porch two nights before, longer now, like there was more worth checking with him not already halfway out the door. "Just clocking that you showed up empty-handed to a meal you weren't invited to."

"I was invited about forty seconds ago."

"That checks out." Sam nodded at the cutting board. "Finish that, then. Since you're apparently earning your seat."

Lucifer picked up the knife and finished what had been left — even strokes, the butter spread edge to edge, nothing wasted. He set the last slice on the tray without being told where the tray was.

Sam noticed that too. He didn't say anything about it, but his eyes went to the tray, then to Lucifer, and stayed a half second longer than the bread required.

They ate around the counter, three stools crowded into a line built for two. Sam served without measuring — plate for Elizabeth first, then Lucifer, then his own, sauce ladled evenly, bread pushed within reach of everyone before he'd sat down himself.

"So where'd you say you were from," Sam said, twirling pasta onto his fork.

"I didn't."

Sam's fork paused halfway up. "Right."

"He's private," Elizabeth said, not looking up from her own plate. "It's a whole thing. Don't make it weird."

"I'm not making anything weird. I'm asking a question at my own dinner table." Sam pointed the fork at Lucifer. "What do you actually do all day, then. Nobody in this town's seen you work a normal job, and it's not that big a town."

"I look for someone."

The fork stopped moving. "Come again?"

"It's a family thing," Elizabeth said quickly, reaching for the bread. "Complicated. Long story, boring in the middle."

Lucifer caught the shift in Sam's face — eyes narrowing a beat after the question left him. He eased back from the edge he hadn't meant to walk to.

"It's not something I explain well," he said. "I'm still working on the short version."

Sam held the look another moment, then let it go with a small shrug that didn't fully commit to believing him. "Fair enough. Everybody's got one of those."

The conversation moved on without him steering it — Sam narrating the sauce recipe at more length than the sauce required, some story about a customer at whatever he did during the day that made Elizabeth laugh a half-beat too quick and too loud. Lucifer ate and watched the two of them work at being fine at each other, neither one asking anything the other might actually have to answer honestly. The postcard on the fridge held its place under a lemon-shaped magnet, corners soft from being taken down and put back more times than a postcard should survive. Neither of them mentioned it. Neither of them needed to.

"You're staring at my fridge," Sam said.

"It's a nice fridge."

"It's a normal fridge." Sam pushed back from the counter, plate empty, and started stacking. "Liz, you good? You barely touched that."

"I ate plenty." Elizabeth was already reaching for his plate too, stacking it onto her own. "It was good. You always make too much."

"That wasn't what I asked."

"I know." She carried both plates to the sink, already turning the tap on before he could say anything else. "I'm good, Sam."

Sam watched her back for a second, towel still over his shoulder, and didn't push it. Lucifer watched him not push it.

---

The porch held the last of the day's heat without the sun behind it to make it unpleasant. Elizabeth sat on the top step; Lucifer stayed standing a step below, close enough to talk without needing to raise anything.

"You do that a lot," he said. "Make it sound smaller than it is."

She glanced over. "Do what."

"Whatever that was, at the table. When he asked if you were all right."

Elizabeth didn't answer right away. She picked at a splinter on the step's edge instead, working it loose with one finger. "It's easier this way."

"I didn't say it wasn't."

She looked at him properly then, something working behind it that didn't resolve into a question. He hadn't meant to say it. It served no version of the assignment, and he had nothing to gain from having said it. He'd said it anyway.

"He worries enough already," she said finally. "About things that are actually his to worry about. I'm not going to hand him one more."

"That's not the same as it being fine."

"No." She let the splinter go. "It's not."

She didn't say anything else after that, and he didn't ask for more. The bulb she'd bought was still sitting on the step below her feet, exactly where it had been every night this week, useless in its sleeve. Neither of them looked at it.

"I should get back," Lucifer said.

"Tristan's waiting?"

"Something like that."

She didn't walk him to the gate this time. She stayed on the step, arms around her knees, and watched him go the same way she had every other night — except this time she didn't say anything as the distance opened up between them, and neither did he.
