# Hell — Lucifer

The knock was Sebastian's — three sharp raps, evenly spaced, too polite for a place that had no use for politeness. Lucifer heard it through the iron and the rock and the low endless murmur of the realm beneath him, and he did not get up.

He lifted one hand from where it rested against the throne's arm and turned it over, lazy, unhurried. The doors — two slabs of black iron taller than any house that had ever stood on Earth, hinges wide enough to fit a man through sideways — took the gesture as an order because that was all they were built to do. They tore open. The sound wasn't a creak. It was a detonation, air displaced so hard it guttered every torch down the length of the hall, and Sebastian came through it not walking but flying, robes snapping, arms out, and landed hard on the black stone ten feet inside the room.

"You could open them yourself," Lucifer said, without looking. "They're not locked."

Sebastian picked himself up with the particular dignity of a man who had done this before and hated it every time. "You know I don't like doing that."

"I know you don't like doing anything that isn't in triplicate." Lucifer's head stayed propped against his fist, elbow on the throne's arm, chin tilted just enough that his eyes could follow Sebastian's approach without the rest of him bothering to move. "Sit. Report. I have places to not be."

The throne room of Hell did not look like anything Earth had ever built a word for. It was carved rather than constructed — walls of black basalt that had been smoothed by nothing so patient as water, veined through with threads of dull red that pulsed, faint and slow, never quite settling into stillness. There were no torches in sconces; the fire lived in the walls themselves, seams of it opening and closing, brightening and dimming with no fixed rhythm. The ceiling was lost somewhere above, dark enough that a man could convince himself there wasn't one at all, that the room simply went up forever into more of the same waiting dark. Ash drifted down sometimes, from nowhere anyone had ever traced, and settled without ever seeming to accumulate.

And in the center of it, close enough to the throne that its lowest branches brushed the black stone steps, grew the tree.

It should not have fit. That was the first thing about it, every time — it was too large for the room and the room made space for it anyway. Its trunk was thick — wider than three men could have spanned side by side — bark a deep, dulled gold, heavier-looking than anything bright. The branches spread wide and climbed high, disappearing into the same dark that hid the ceiling, hung with leaves that never yellowed and never fell, and threaded among the leaves, close and heavy, the fruit — round, full, a deep wet red. It had come down with him. Nobody had planted it here. It had simply been growing in the throne room the first time he'd stood in it, six thousand years ago and more, and it had never once left.

He did not look at it often. He didn't need to. He always knew exactly where it was.

Sebastian settled onto the low bench that had been carried in for exactly this purpose years ago and unrolled a length of parchment that had clearly been rolled and unrolled a great many times that day already. His hands were not quite steady. Lucifer noticed — he'd been noticing things like this about Sebastian for two weeks now — filed it, didn't comment on it, because commenting only ever made him worse.

"Third sector's stable," Sebastian said. "Fourth sector's stable. Fifth had a containment flare Tuesday, nothing structural, we handled it." He cleared his throat. It was a small sound and he made it three times in the space of a single sentence. "Sixth sector reports normal. Seventh —"

"You've read me the same four sectors every week for a year," Lucifer said. "You don't usually clear your throat like you're about to confess to something."

"I'm not confessing to anything." Too fast. He knew it the moment it left his mouth, Lucifer could see him know it, watch the muscle in his jaw tighten around the words, a beat too late to call them back. "Seventh sector reports normal," he said again, steadier. "First sector —"

Above them, without any sound to announce it, one of the fruit let go of its branch.

It fell straight and unhurried, angled to land square on the crown of Sebastian's bowed head — and Lucifer, still not looking up, still with his chin propped on his fist and his eyes on the middle distance of the report Sebastian was holding, lifted two fingers an inch off the throne's arm. The apple bent its fall midair, sudden and smooth, and came instead to rest in Lucifer's open palm. He turned it once between his fingers, weighed it, set it down beside him on the stone without eating it and without looking at it, the whole gesture taking no real attention at all.

Sebastian had flinched — shoulders up around his ears, a full-body jolt — before he caught himself and smoothed it into something that might, from a distance, have passed for a cough.

"First sector," he said, a third time, and this time his voice cracked on the word *first.*

Lucifer's eyes came off the middle distance and settled on him. "You've said that word three times like it owes you money."

"It's fine," Sebastian said. "It's — everything in first sector is within normal parameters. I checked twice before I came up." He said it with the flat conviction of someone who had decided, by force of will, to believe it. "I always check twice."

"You check once. You've checked twice on exactly nothing in the eleven years I've had you doing this job."

Sebastian opened his mouth to answer that, and didn't get the chance.

The doors — already hanging open, still smoking faintly along one hinge where the iron had taken the earlier abuse — banged wide a second time, no gesture behind it this time, just a body hitting them at a dead sprint. A lesser demon, one of the runners from the lower levels, skidded the last ten feet across the black stone on his knees and didn't bother standing before he spoke, chest heaving so hard the words came out in pieces.

"My lord — sector one — the containment on Eve's cell — it's weakening. It's been weakening for an hour, we didn't catch it until — the wall's not holding, my lord, it's —"

Lucifer was already standing.

He did it without any of the theater the doors had gotten a minute earlier. One moment he was draped across the throne, chin on his fist, bored down to the bone. The next he was on his feet at the top of the stairs, and something in the room changed — the air gone taut, a silence dropping in under the low endless murmur of the realm, sudden and absolute.

He didn't ask questions. There wasn't time to ask questions, and he already knew, with the flat certainty of a man who has spent six thousand years learning the exact shape of every disaster that could happen to him, what an hour of unwatched weakening in that particular cell meant.

He reached out — not with his hand, with something under the hand, something that had no name because names were for things that had edges — and took hold of the realm's time, gripped it taut, and pulled it slow.

It did not stop. It never fully stopped; Hell did not allow for stillness any more than it allowed for silence. But it thickened, slow and heavy, until the ash drifting down from the dark overhead hung nearly suspended, until Sebastian's flinch — he was flinching again, he'd started before the runner even finished his sentence — stretched out into something that would take him a full minute of real time to complete. The fire in the walls guttered at a quarter of its speed. Somewhere behind him he heard the runner's next word begin and never quite arrive.

Lucifer walked down the throne steps at his own pace, through all of it, and opened the floor.

He didn't need a door for this. He tore one instead, a long jagged seam splitting the black basalt from the foot of the throne outward, widening as it went, a ravine opening down into a dark that had no bottom he'd ever bothered finding because he'd never needed to go this deep this fast. He didn't hesitate at the edge. He stepped into it and let himself fall.

The fall took a long time. It always did, going down — coming back up was the part he could hurry. The walls of the ravine slid past him too fast to be walls and too close to be sky, red light showing through cracks in the rock as he passed sector after sector, each one a burst of heat and noise and then gone, lost in the next stretch of dark. The air screamed past him, hot and then hotter, and somewhere in the middle of that long drop he stopped being able to tell whether the roaring in his ears was the wind or his own pulse.

He hit the bottom of sector one already running.

The cell block was chaos — the particular chaos of a place built never to move, now moving — dust sifting from the ceiling in sheets, one whole wall of ancient reinforcement cracked clean through, and directly ahead of him, hanging in the ruptured air where the cell's fourth wall should have been solid stone, a portal.

It wasn't like his own. His tore and closed clean, sealing shut again the instant he was through. This one shimmered wrong at the edges, sickly, its border ragged and uneven, and through the center of it he could see her.

Eve.

She was most of the way through already — shoulders gone, one arm gone, disappearing into whatever waited on the other side of that torn place in the world at a pace that should have been instant and instead, dragged down into the same slow crawl of time he'd pulled over the whole realm, was agonizingly, unbearably slow. He caught her eyes across the space between them. Six thousand years he had not looked into those eyes and he knew them instantly.

She was reaching for him.

Not away from him, into the portal that was swallowing her. Toward him. One hand still on this side of the tear, fingers spread, straining back across a gap that time had made enormous, and he could not have said afterward what he read in her face — plea or command, rescue or trap, he had no time to sort it and no time left to try.

He ran.

The distance should have been nothing. Ten strides, less. He closed it fast, fast enough that each step landed before the last one's weight had settled, and he was almost there, almost close enough to close his hand around hers, when he understood the gap wasn't going to close in time no matter how fast he was — and he threw himself the last of it, left the ground entirely, every part of him stretched toward the last inch of her fingers —

— and the portal took her anyway. Closed on empty air a heartbeat after her hand vanished through it, snapping shut with a sound too small, too distant, for what it meant.

Time let go all at once.

It came back in a rush, and every ounce of force he'd thrown into that last stride arrived with it. He had nothing left to jump into — no portal, no Eve, no target at all — and the momentum carried him straight into the cell's inner wall at a speed that should have killed anything less than what he was. The impact cratered the stone, a deep, clean divot punched into rock that had stood since before there were rocks to compare it to, and he slid down out of it to his knees on the broken floor, dust and grit raining down around him, ears ringing with the aftershock of his own speed.

He knelt there a moment. Just a moment. Long enough to catch a breath he didn't technically need and didn't take anyway.

Then he stood, and snapped his fingers once.

Two shapes rose out of the shadowed corners of the ruined cell block — Sebastian first, arriving from the throne room already breathless, having clearly run the whole way rather than wait for a summons that hadn't come yet; and beside him, unhurried, straightening out of the dark with the composure of someone who had never once in her existence been caught flat-footed, Lillith.

"She's gone," Lucifer said, before either of them could ask. "Portal opened in her cell wall. I got here as fast as I could pull it — I had the whole realm slowed to nothing and it still wasn't enough. She's out."

Sebastian's face went bloodless. "Out — out where, out to —"

"I don't know yet." Lucifer's jaw worked once, and something in his voice, underneath the flatness, had gone very cold and very quiet. "That's the problem."

Lillith didn't gasp, didn't reach for a wall to steady herself the way Sebastian half did. She simply went still in the particular way that meant she was recalculating everything she'd assumed that morning, fast, and would have new orders of her own ready before he finished giving his. "How long was the wall compromised before anyone caught it?"

"An hour. Maybe longer." He crossed to the ruptured cell wall — what was left of it — and crouched at its base, and that was when he saw the marks low along the stone, dark and dried and old, so old they'd gone the color of the rock itself and would have been invisible to anyone who wasn't looking for exactly this. Not one gouge. Hundreds of them, layered over each other, worn into a groove by repetition stretched across more time than made any sense. Fingernails, most likely, at the start. Something harder, later, when the nails had worn away and grown back and worn away again.

She had been clawing at that wall the entire time she'd been in it. Millennia of it, more patience than any human mind could hold, and no one — not him, not the wards, not the watch rotation Sebastian swore up and down was airtight — had caught the wall thinning until it was already too late to matter.

He stood back up slowly.

"Lillith. You're in charge until I get back. Full authority — everything I'd sign off on, you sign off on, no waiting for me." He didn't say *if I get back*, though it sat under the sentence anyway, and none of them acknowledged it out loud. "Sebastian. Every hand you've got, on every cell in sector one, tonight. I want every wall in this block reinforced before the next watch change, and I want to know how long this one's been thinning before I'm back to ask you in person."

Sebastian's throat worked. "Where are you going?"

"Up," Lucifer said. "And then out."

He didn't wait for either of them to answer. He turned back toward the ravine — still open above him, a long scar of paler dark against the black — and jumped, and the climb up was nothing like the fall down had been. He didn't fall up so much as he simply arrived, faster with every level, sector by sector blurring past in reverse, and behind him, as he rose, the ravine sealed itself shut, the scar in the basalt closing floor by floor until by the time he stepped back out into his own throne room there was no seam left in the stone at all, no sign anything had ever torn through it.

The throne room was empty and silent and exactly as he'd left it, the apple he'd caught still sitting where he'd set it down on the arm of the throne, the tree standing over all of it, patient and enormous, unmoved by the last several minutes — several lifetimes, by the reckoning of the men who'd have to reinforce that wall tonight.

He walked to the tree.

He reached up into the branches and closed his hand around one of the fruit, not the one he'd caught, a fresh one, heavy and warm — the same warmth it had carried on the branch in a garden he hadn't seen with his own eyes in longer than most of Earth's history had existed. He didn't eat it. He just held it, turning it once in his palm, and tipped his head back toward the dark that had no ceiling, and spoke.

"I need to go to Earth."

He didn't shout it. He never had to. Whatever was listening had always been listening, whether or not He chose to answer.

The silence that came back wasn't empty. It had weight to it, the stillness of something choosing whether to speak. When the voice came, it didn't come from any one direction. It simply filled the space — shaped, unlike the realm's ceaseless murmur, and carrying warmth underneath something that was not quite warm.

*You know what she is to you.*

"I know what she did tonight," Lucifer said. "She's out. However she managed it — however long she's been managing it — she's loose on Earth, and every hour I stand here having this conversation is another hour she's had to run." He turned the fruit once more in his hand, didn't look at it. "Send me after her. I'll bring her back."

*You may go.*

He hadn't expected it to be that simple, and it wasn't.

*You will not use what you are while you're there. No divinity — not for this, not to make it faster, not even to save yourself the trouble of a locked door. You will keep every commandment you were given the first time you walked among them, the same as any of Mine who's gone down before you. And you will not go alone.*

"I don't need —"

*Tristan will be there.* Something in the voice, saying his brother's chosen name, carried an edge Lucifer recognized from a very long time ago and hadn't heard aimed at him in centuries. *He'll know why. You won't need to explain it to him, and you won't try to talk him out of watching you. He's not there to help you catch her, Lucifer. He's there to make sure you come back the same shape you left in.*

"I don't need a keeper."

*You have one anyway.*

"She's had thousands of years to plan whatever this is, and I get sent down there with my hands tied and my brother standing over my shoulder like I'm the one who broke out of a cell." The fruit was warm and heavy in his fist and he was gripping it harder than he meant to, hard enough that had it been anything but what it was, it would have burst. "That's not a hunt. That's a leash with extra steps."

*It's the only way you go at all. Argue it as long as you like — the terms don't move.*

He argued it at length and with no expectation of winning, because some part of him had never stopped believing that if he just found the right words this time, six thousand years of the same answer might finally bend. It didn't bend. It never had. The voice let him finish — its patience somehow worse than if it had simply cut him off — and when he ran out of things to say, the silence that followed had already made up its mind before he opened his mouth the first time.

The floor at his feet split open.

Not the ravine this time — this tore differently, cleaner, edges settling almost at once into something steady instead of raw, the rough scar of his own doors closing giving way to a portal with none of the sickened, uneven look of the one that had taken Eve. This one held its shape from the first instant, steady. It didn't flicker. It didn't strain at its edges. It simply waited, open, for him to step through it.

He looked back once at the tree — gold branches disappearing up into the dark, red fruit hanging heavy, the same tree it had always been, waiting in the same room it had waited in since the day he'd first arrived here with nothing left of himself worth keeping — and then he stepped into the portal his Father had opened for him, fruit still closed in his fist, and let it take him.

It closed behind him without a sound.
