The coffee maker is doing its slow, choking thing again, the one where it sounds like it's drowning before it commits to actually brewing. I give it a minute, then give the counter a wipe-down it doesn't need, because standing there watching a machine suffer feels worse than pretending to be useful.

There are two bowls in the sink from last night that neither of us washed. I do them anyway, lukewarm water, no gloves, and tell myself it counts as waking up.

"Please tell me that's for me," Sam says from the doorway, hair doing nothing anyone would call a style, and reaches past my shoulder for a mug before the coffee's even finished.

"It's for whoever gets to it first. That's the system."

"Rigged system. You're standing right here."

"I was here first. That's not rigged, that's called winning."

He bumps the counter with his hip on his way to the fridge, which is as close as he gets to affection before nine in the morning, and asks if I slept. I say fine, because fine is easier than the real answer and isn't exactly a lie — I did sleep, eventually, which is most of what fine means these days. He doesn't push. He never pushes as hard as he could; that's just him looking out for me, backwards as it is, even if I'd never say that to his face.

"You've got that face," he says anyway, pouring his coffee into the mug he just stole.

"I have one face. It's the only one I own."

"The one where you're thinking and not telling me what."

"I'm thinking about whether we have more eggs or if that's wishful thinking."

He lets it go, because that's the deal we have without ever discussing it: I get to keep the parts of my own head that are mine, and he gets to pretend he believed the eggs thing.

I check the fridge. We do not, in fact, have more eggs.

I make a short list on the pad stuck to the fridge with a magnet shaped like a lemon — eggs, the refill for my head, the thing that stopped my alarm clock from working — and decide the pharmacy can be first, since it's the one errand with an actual hour attached to it before they close for lunch like it's still 1950.

"I'll come," Sam says, already reaching for his keys like it's decided.

"I can walk four blocks by myself, Sam."

"Didn't say you couldn't."

"You were reaching for your keys."

"Reflex." He sets them back down, slower than the reach, like it costs him something small. "Go. Bring home eggs. Don't talk to strangers."

"I'm nineteen, not nine."

"Rule still applies."

Outside it's already thick, flat heat, sitting on the whole town by nine a.m. in August, hot enough that the crosswalk paint looks ready to peel off the asphalt on its own. I cut across the square instead of around it, past the fountain where the Henderson twins are doing something with a water gun that's technically banned, past the bench where Mrs. Alvarez reads the same paperback she's been reading all summer, and lift a hand at her. She lifts one back without looking up.

The pharmacy is cool in the specific, slightly medical way pharmacies always are, and smells like band-aids and air conditioning. The woman at the counter — I don't know her name, just her face, the one with the reading glasses always pushed up into her hair instead of on her nose — rings up my refill and the clock-radio battery I grabbed off a rack I wasn't planning on browsing, and tells me to have a good one. I tell her the same.

It's on the walk back, bag swinging against my leg, that I decide against the square again. There's a cut-through behind the row of shops, a service alley I've used a hundred times to shave two minutes off getting home, mostly empty dumpsters and the backs of buildings nobody bothers to paint. It's quieter back there than the square ever gets. That's usually the whole appeal.

I'm maybe halfway down it when something at the far end stops me without asking permission first — nothing I could name in the half-second before my feet already stopped moving. Just motion that's wrong for the hour, wrong for the street, too fast and too close together.

There's a man down there, past the loading dock of the old hardware store, and he is not alone.

I count wrong at first — too many bodies moving at once to get a real number — but it's more than two against him, maybe more than three. They're on him from different sides, turning between them fast enough to meet each one before it lands, and none of them are using anything I'd call a weapon, no glint of metal, nothing swung that makes a sound a bat or a pipe would make. It's all hands and bodies and a noise that reaches me a half-second late each time, a flat, wet sound I feel more than hear.

It doesn't look like anything choreographed. Choreographed has a rhythm to it, even bad choreographed, even movies I've seen that didn't spend much on the fight. This has no rhythm. It's ugly and fast, rougher than anything I've ever watched on a screen, and some older, dumber part of my brain understands that before the rest of me catches up.

I should be more scared than I am, standing here. I register that distantly, a fact, not yet a feeling. What I actually feel is closer to the thing I always feel around something I can't explain: a pull toward it instead of away, a want to understand what I'm looking at even while every other part of me is yelling that understanding isn't the assignment right now.

He's outnumbered badly. That part isn't ambiguous. What is ambiguous — what snags wrong, more than the violence does — is that he doesn't look like a man who's losing. He looks almost bored by it, like someone swatting at something more annoying than dangerous, and that wrongness is worse to look at than the fight itself.

I flinch when one of them goes down hard against the dumpster, a sound that lands wrong, too heavy for a body, and in the second it takes me to make myself look again, the shape of the whole thing has changed. It happens faster than a blink, too fast to catch the actual moment it turned. When I look back there's only him standing in the alley, and the others are — gone, or down, or something in between that I can't resolve from here and don't get close enough to ask.

He doesn't look hurt. He doesn't look like anything happened to him at all.

I don't wait to find out which it is. Backing away feels smarter than running, at least at first — quieter, less likely to turn me into something worth noticing — and it's only once there's a corner between us that I let myself move faster, pulse going harder than the walk should account for. Whether he saw me is a question I don't look back long enough to answer. I tell myself that's a choice and not just fear doing the choosing for me.

By the time I hit the square again everything is exactly the way I left it not long ago — the twins, the water gun, Mrs. Alvarez and her paperback — which feels, for one disorienting second, wrong. Nobody out here even slowed down.

Sam's at the kitchen table when I get in, and he asks if they had the eggs after all, and I say I forgot the eggs, which is true and also the smallest true thing I have to offer him right now. He doesn't clock anything off in my voice. I'm good at that, or he's bad at this, and either way it buys me the room to not say the rest of it out loud, not yet, maybe not ever, the same way I don't hand him the other things I carry around on an ordinary Tuesday.

I put the pharmacy bag on the counter. My hands aren't quite steady doing it. I notice, and there's nothing useful to do about it, so I don't.

What I keep landing on, underneath the shaking, isn't fear so much as something flatter and more stubborn: I am not about to let twenty seconds in an alley I chose to walk through turn into a reason to stop walking through alleys, stop cutting through my own town, because it's mine too. I've lived here my whole life. Whatever that was back there doesn't get to be bigger than that just because I don't have a word for it yet.

I go back out onto the porch anyway, bag of eggs I don't actually have yet still just a line on a list, and sit on the step like it's any other morning, because it still technically is one.
