# Rhythm — Elizabeth

Most of the color was already gone from the sky by the time she noticed them outside — just a thin band of rust low over the Hendersons' roofline, the rest of it gone to the flat blue-grey that meant the streetlights would be doing the real work soon. She'd been at the kitchen window rinsing a glass when the shape of two men on the sidewalk registered as something other than strangers.

She left the glass in the rack and went out.

Tristan was saying something to Adrian in a low voice she couldn't catch, arms crossed, the posture of a man who had somewhere else to be and was choosing, for the moment, not to say so. He caught sight of her coming down the walk and didn't stop talking so much as finish faster.

"Evening." Tristan said it in the tone he used for everyone who wasn't his brother — polite, efficient, giving nothing away.

"You're not staying." It wasn't really a question, and she didn't dress it up as one.

"I have work."

"He always has work," Adrian said.

"Someone has to." Tristan looked at his brother a second longer than the sentence needed, said nothing further about it, and turned to go. He didn't say goodbye to either of them specifically — just lifted a hand as he went, already three steps into leaving.

She watched him round the corner before she looked back at Adrian. "Does he ever actually relax?"

"Not that I've seen."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It's efficient." He said it flatly enough that she couldn't tell if he meant it as a joke, which was becoming, she'd noticed, one of his more reliable qualities.

They stood there a moment on the emptying sidewalk, neither of them moving toward her porch or his usual step. The stillness of the night made staying outside feel less chosen than inevitable.

"I don't feel like sitting tonight." She glanced at him. "Walk?"

"Where."

"The park. It's not far." She was already moving, which answered the question better than anything she could have said.

He fell in beside her without further comment, hands in his pockets, matching her pace the way he always did — not behind her, not ahead, exactly level. He'd settled on this as the correct way to walk next to someone a long time ago and never once reconsidered it.

The park was two streets over, close enough that they were through the gate before either of them had said much of anything. The lamps along the main path came on while they were crossing the grass toward it — the first one, then the next, then the whole line of them at once, catching the dark before it could really settle. She'd never actually watched that happen before. It made the place look staged, briefly, before it settled back into being the park again.

"That's convenient timing."

"It's not for us."

"I know that. I'm allowed to enjoy it anyway."

They walked. The path curved past the fountain, dry for the season, and on toward the far end where the trees thickened and the lamps thinned out to almost nothing. She let the quiet sit for a while, because it was the kind of quiet that didn't need filling, and because she'd learned that he answered better when a question hadn't been sitting in wait for him.

"You haven't said anything about her in a while." She let the sentence settle before she went on. "The woman. I realized I couldn't remember the last time you brought her up on your own."

"I hadn't noticed."

"That's not really an answer."

He walked a few more steps before he said anything else, which by now she recognized as him deciding something rather than avoiding it. "There isn't much new to say. We've been at this a long time. She's good at not being found — she always has been. I don't think there's a version of this where it happens quickly."

"How long is a long time?"

"Longer than you'd believe if I told you the number."

"Try me."

"Not tonight." He said it without any edge to it — not a refusal, just not yet. "Ask me something specific. I'll tell you what I actually know."

She thought about it. "What does she look like? You've never said."

"I could describe her and it wouldn't help you. She won't look the way I remember her — she's had a long time to become someone else on purpose." He shrugged, unbothered by the admission.

"Is that a real answer, or another version of a non-answer?"

"Both, probably."

She let that sit, filed rather than pressed. "Okay. What's she actually like, then? Not what she looks like — what she's like."

Something in his face closed, gently and completely. "That's not something I can get into right now."

He didn't stop walking. If anything he picked the pace up slightly, nodding at nothing in particular toward the tree line ahead, and she understood well enough that the subject had ended itself. She didn't ask again. She'd learned that lesson with him a long time before tonight — that a closed door, unlike an unanswered one, didn't reopen for more knocking.

"Tell me about Tristan, then." She nudged the subject sideways. "You never talk about him either, but at least he's standing right in front of me half the time."

That one, unexpectedly, got a real answer. "Before all this — before Earth, before any of it — he kept the Garden. Watched over it. It was his to run, day and night, and he was good at it. He loved it more than he's ever loved anything since, I think, including this job he's stuck with now."

"Tristan. A gardener." She tried to picture it and the picture wouldn't hold — him with dirt under his nails, patient with something growing too slowly to notice. "I can't see it."

"You'd have seen it if you'd known him then. He used to talk to the trees like they owed him updates."

"Please tell me that's true."

"It's true." The corner of his mouth moved, not quite all the way into anything. "He took it seriously. Everyone did. It wasn't a chore to any of us — it was just what the days were."

"What was it like? Before all — this." She gestured at the park, the town, the general shape of everything, not sure herself what she meant by it, and he seemed to understand anyway.

He was quiet long enough that she thought he might close this one off too. Then he didn't.

"We used to sit around the Tree of Life, all of us, and just — be there. Eat, mostly. There was fruit on it that tasted like whatever you needed it to taste like that day, which sounds like a strange thing to build a memory around, but it wasn't really about the fruit. It was that everyone showed up. Every single time, without being asked. My brothers, my sisters — we'd sit out there for what felt like whole days and laugh about nothing. Nobody had anywhere else to be. Nobody had a reason to leave early." He said it plainly, but something under the plainness had gone warm, animated in a register she hadn't heard from him before. Whatever he was remembering was still bright to him, even after everything that came after. "It was the easiest thing in the world. I didn't understand at the time how rare that actually was."

"That sounds nice." She meant it, and something in how quietly she said it seemed to let him keep going.

"It was. And then one day —" He stopped walking. Not dramatically — he simply ran out of the next word. "One day he didn't come. God. He didn't come to the gathering. First time in — I don't know how long. He'd been spending most of his nights somewhere else by then, thinking about whether to make something new. People. Humans." The word cost him something small and specific to say. "That was the first time any of us noticed anything had changed."

He didn't say anything after that. He kept his eyes fixed on the path in front of them, though nothing about it had changed, and his hands had gone very still in his pockets.

She didn't ask what happened next. She could see, without being told, that whatever came after that gathering wasn't a story he had any intention of finishing tonight — maybe not any night. The stopping itself had cost him something; she could see it in the set of his jaw, the half second too long before he started walking again. She let it be.

There was a bench a little further along, half in the light and half out of it, and she steered them toward it without making a thing of it. He sat when she sat, which wasn't a given with him, and for a while neither of them said anything at all.

She put a hand on his arm, not asking permission, just there. He didn't pull away from it. After a moment he let his head come to rest against her shoulder, and stayed like that, not saying anything, not needing to. She didn't say anything either. It wasn't the start of something — it was exactly what it was: someone who'd run out of the words he'd been willing to spend, choosing to be quiet next to a person instead of alone with it.

They stayed that way long enough that a couple with a stroller passed twice on the same loop.

"What do you like to do?" He sat back up. "When you're not doing this?"

She laughed, a little surprised by the question landing exactly when it did. "That's a big shift."

"You don't have to answer."

"No, I like it." She thought about where to start. "Sophie, probably, more than anything else. My best friend — you haven't met her yet, but you will. We've known each other since I was in third grade. She was a year ahead of me, fourth grade, and I have no idea why she decided I was worth the time, but she did, and that was it."

"What does that mean, exactly? 'That was it.'"

"It means we've basically been the same unit ever since. We used to walk all over town — every shop that would let two kids under ten hang around without buying anything, we found it. There's a bookstore on Fifth that still gives us a discount, partly because we used to be in there so much they probably felt bad charging us full price, and partly because Sophie makes literally every person who works retail fall in love with her within about four minutes. It's not fair, honestly. I've watched her do it to strangers."

"That sounds like a useful skill."

"It's an unbelievably useful skill. We'd watch movies — every terrible rom-com either of us could get our hands on, we'd rate them afterward like we were actual critics, very serious about it. We used to come here, actually. This park. We had a whole system for the swings that made no sense to anyone but us." She looked around for the swing set, half-expecting it to still be exactly where it had been. "We still do half of this, honestly. We're grown women and we still argue about which one of the leads in a movie made the worse decision."

"She sounds like family."

"She is family. That's the thing — she's basically my sister at this point, not just my friend. She started dating my brother our junior year of high school, if you can believe that, and they're still together. Still going strong. She's been at every birthday, every bad day, every everything, for longer than I can even track anymore. The three of us try to do things together whenever we actually can. It's — " She stopped, hunting for the word, and gave up trying to be precise about it. "It's the best part of my life, probably. If I had to pick one."

He was quiet for a second. Then, dry, almost too quiet to catch: "A bookstore discount earned through sheer charm. That's a very specific kind of power."

She laughed before she'd fully processed that he'd made a joke — a real one, on purpose, aimed and everything. "Was that a joke? Did you just make a joke?"

"No."

"You did. Oh my god, you smile and you tell jokes now. Who even are you?"

Something pulled at the corner of his mouth, close enough to real that she almost caught the whole thing arriving — and then it stopped, cut off clean. He looked away from her, out toward the dark end of the path, and didn't say anything for a moment.

She let it go. For just that second, he seemed to have wandered somewhere he hadn't meant to go, and needed a moment to find his way back — she didn't want to be the reason he felt watched doing it. She looked at her phone instead, mostly for something to do with her eyes.

The time on the screen made her sit up. "Oh — I should get back. Sam's going to be pacing by now."

"I'll walk you."

They went back the way they'd come, past the fountain, past the lamps thinning out again as the path narrowed toward the gate, and neither of them filled the walk with much of anything — just the ordinary noise of two people who'd already said the real things for the night and didn't need to manufacture more.

At her door she stepped inside first, one hand still on the frame. "Goodnight," she said, and lifted her hand in something between a wave and a wait-a-second.

She glanced down for a moment, checking the lock was set the way it always stuck if she didn't push it, and when she looked back up to say something else, the sidewalk was empty. No gate sound, no footsteps fading down the block, nothing. Just Adrian, gone, in the time it had taken her to look at a doorknob.

She stood there a second, glancing back down the empty sidewalk once more before she let it go. He probably just moved fast. People did that all the time — you looked away for half a second and missed the whole exit.

She closed the door and went to find Sam.
