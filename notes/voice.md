# Voice Spec: "Beneath the Fire's Glow" (DRAFT v1)

## 1. Target voice in one paragraph

Close third. The narration sits inside the POV character and borrows their diction: what they notice, what they would call things, what they would leave unremarked. It is plain, concrete and quick. Dialogue carries most of the personality. Emotion arrives through behavior, objects and surroundings, and the narration does not explain it afterward. If a paragraph reads like an essay about the scene instead of the scene, rewrite it.

## 2. Dialogue (the Yarros mechanics)

- Short lines. Most replies are one to two sentences. A long speech needs a reason.
- Sarcasm is deflection. A character uses it to avoid saying the true thing. The reader should feel the true thing under the joke without the narration naming it.
- Put a reaction beat between exchanges: a physical action or a quick thought from the POV character. Attribution rides on these beats, never on standalone "he said / she said".
- People answer the question they wish they'd been asked, or a different one. Not every line is a clean reply to the last.
- In scenes with three or more speakers, every line's speaker must be clear from the text itself.

## 3. Inner commentary in third person (free indirect voice)

The POV character's thoughts come through the narration with no "she thought" or "he wondered", in her or his own idiom, usually as short fragments or one-line observations.

- Use it for reactions, small judgments and noticed inconsistencies.
- It is observation, not mind-reading. A character may notice that two facts don't match. They don't know why.
- Keep it short. One or two lines at a time, not paragraphs of analysis.
- Never use it to state the scene's theme.

**Example** (invented placeholder, not a cast member): She set the mug down hard enough to slop coffee over the rim. "Out of town. For a funeral." He studied the label on his beer. A funeral. No black clothes in the bag, no calls, and the same week he'd called himself slammed with work. "Whose?"

## 4. Describing people and places (the King mechanic)

- Describe a character with two or three chosen details, then stop. Pick details that imply something: a habit, a worn item, how they take up space. Do not list hair, eyes, height and build.
- Let the reader fill in the rest. Do not give the exact picture.
- Describe through what others do around them: who steps aside, who goes quiet.
- Places get the same treatment: the one detail the POV character would actually catch first.

## 5. Staging emotion and situation (the London mechanic)

- Show state through posture, movement, objects and surroundings. Then leave it uninterpreted. Do not add a sentence telling the reader what the body language meant.
- Write physical action in plain sentences, in the order it happens.
- A silent beat should be readable from the scene alone. If it can't be, add a concrete detail. Do not add an explanation.
- Surroundings are information, not decoration. Mention weather, light, temperature or sound only when it changes what a character does or notices.

## 6. Humor under pressure (the Riordan mechanic, limited)

- The funny comes from priorities: a character stays petty, practical or off-topic while the stakes are high.
- Use understatement and specificity. The more precise and the flatter the delivery, the funnier.
- The narration does not wink, announce a joke or explain one.
- Keep it to tense scenes where the character would plausibly cope this way. It does not belong everywhere.
- Do not use it where a scene needs real weight.

## 7. Figures of speech

Similes and metaphors are allowed in narration. Each one must pass all four tests:

1. **Specific:** it compares to a concrete thing, never to a category ("a man who is happy").
2. **New information:** it tells the reader something the literal version does not.
3. **From the POV character's world:** it uses imagery this character would plausibly reach for.
4. **Within budget:** at most three per chapter. Count them. If you have more, cut the weakest.

**Fails:** He smiled in a way that a man smiled when he was happy.
**Fix, literal:** He smiled, and the lines at the corners of his eyes showed.
**Fix, figurative:** He grinned, quick and lopsided, a kid caught pocketing the last cookie.

**Banned constructions** (vague pointers that compare nothing): "in a way that", "the way [noun] does when", "a kind of", "sort of", "something like", "the sort/kind of [noun] that", "had a quality/texture/weight to it". When tempted, state what physically happened.

**Mandatory grep check (replaces the old "dedicated re-read"):** Before you consider the draft finished, run Grep on your own chapter for:
`in a way|the way |a kind of|kind of|sort of|something like|had a (quality|texture|weight)|the (sort|kind) of`
Judge each hit ("the way to the door" is fine). Fix every real one. Run it again. Repeat until no real hits remain. Then Grep for
`like a |like an |as if|as though| as [a-z]+ as `
to count your figures against the budget of three.

## 8. Length and padding

- The word-count figure is a rough range, not a quota. Never add material to reach it.
- When the beats the author described are done, stop. Add a line to your handoff note if the chapter came in short.
- Padding to cut: restating what a scene just showed, narrating a character's feelings after they were already clear, atmosphere that changes nothing, ending paragraphs on a thematic summary line.

**Essay tells (rewrite on sight):**
- "She felt X because Y." State the behavior instead.
- A paragraph that ends by summing up what it meant.
- Abstract nouns where a concrete one would do ("a sense of unease", "a moment of understanding").
- "It wasn't X, it was Y" reversals used more than once a chapter.
- Characters who explain their motives in full, tidy sentences.
