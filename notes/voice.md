## 1. Target voice

First person. The narrator is a specific person telling this moment in their own idiom: what they notice first, what they would call things, what they leave unsaid. The prose is plain, concrete and quick. Dialogue carries a lot of the personality, and the narrator's private commentary carries the rest. The reader gets two things the third-person draft could not: what the narrator thinks, and what the narrator is not saying out loud. Emotion arrives through behavior and specific thought, not through explanation afterward. If a paragraph reads like the narrator writing an essay about their feelings, rewrite it.

## 2. Whose head you are in

- You know only what this narrator perceives, remembers, infers or is told. No head-hopping.
- Other people's feelings are inferred from behavior and may be wrong.
- No hindsight narration ("I didn't know then that…"). No addressing the reader.
- Tense is present. Memories and backstory use past tense and stay brief. No specific durations (see the agent file's elapsed-time rule).
- Do not start more than two sentences in a row with "I". Vary the opening.
- The narrator's name for each person is the name they would actually use. See the agent file's naming rules.

## 3. The inner life: emotional and intellectual

This is why the book is in first person, so it has to be done well. The inner voice has two layers.

**Emotional layer.** What the narrator feels, in their own words, in the moment.
- State it in the narrator's idiom and make it specific: what the feeling made them do or want to do.
- Often the feeling is the opposite of what they say aloud. The contrast between dialogue and thought is the main engine. A sarcastic line out loud and a flat honest one in the head is a Yarros move worth using often.
- Avoid stock phrases: "a wave of", "couldn't help but", "heart hammered", "a breath I didn't know I was holding".

**Intellectual layer.** What the narrator is working out.
- Ordinary thinking, not analysis: an opinion, a half-formed thought, a want, a distraction, a guess that may be wrong. Most thoughts are small and tied to what the narrator sees, smells, wants or feels. Not lectures and not themes.
- For Elizabeth, the noticing habit shows up as a question she asks out loud when something doesn't add up. It is a reflex, not a running analysis. She does not keep a mental ledger, and she misses things. Do not give any narrator running totals, estimates, tallies or deductions unless the author's paragraph calls for it.
- Watch the balance. Most of the inner voice is feeling, wanting, noticing and mild distraction. Reasoning is the smaller share.

**Rules for both layers**
- Every reflection is triggered by something in the scene and changes what the narrator does next or what the reader knows. If it does neither, cut it.
- Keep reflections to one to three sentences at a time. No paragraphs of analysis.
- Never sum up the scene's meaning. Never end a paragraph on a thematic line.
- The narrator can be wrong. A wrong guess, corrected later, is better than a perfect one.

## 4. Dialogue (the Yarros mechanics)

- Short lines. Most replies are one to two sentences. A long speech needs a reason.
- Sarcasm is deflection: a character uses it to avoid the true thing. In first person the narrator may show the true thing in a brief aside.
- A reaction beat sits between exchanges: a physical action or a quick thought from the narrator. Attribution rides on these beats, never on standalone "he said / she said".
- People answer the question they wish they'd been asked, or a different one.
- In scenes with three or more speakers, every line's speaker must be clear from the text itself.

## 5. Describing people and places (the King mechanic)

- Describe a character with two or three chosen details, then stop. Pick details that imply something: a habit, a worn item, how they take up space. Do not list hair, eyes, height and build.
- What the narrator notices first says something about the narrator. Let it.
- Leave the rest for the reader to fill in.
- Places get the same treatment: the one detail the narrator would actually catch first.

## 6. Staging emotion and situation (the London mechanic)

- Show other people's state through posture, movement, objects and surroundings.
- Report what the narrator sees. Do not add a sentence decoding it unless the narrator is reasoning from evidence and could be wrong (see section 3).
- A silent beat should be readable from the scene alone. If it isn't, add a concrete detail. Do not add an explanation.
- Surroundings are information, not decoration. Mention weather, light, temperature or sound only when it changes what a character does or notices.

## 7. Humor under pressure (the Riordan mechanic, limited)

- First person is where this works best: the narrator's priorities stay petty, practical or off-topic while the stakes are high.
- Use understatement and specificity. Flat delivery is funnier than a flourish.
- Narrators differ. Do not make every narrator funny. Use humor only where the narrator's voice card makes it plausible.
- Keep humor out of scenes that need real weight.

## 8. Figures of speech

Similes and metaphors are allowed in narration **and in dialogue — there is no exemption for spoken lines.** Every figure, spoken or narrated, must pass all four tests:

1. **Specific:** it compares to a concrete thing, never to a category ("a man who is happy").
2. **New information:** it tells the reader something the literal version does not.
3. **From this narrator's world:** it uses imagery this narrator would actually reach for. A figure that belongs to another character's life fails.
4. **Within budget:** at most three per chapter, counting both narration and dialogue together. Count them. If you have more, cut the weakest.

Fails: I smile in a way that a person smiles when they're happy.
Fix, literal: I smile, and my face stays that way longer than I mean it to.
Fix, figurative: I grin, quick and lopsided, a kid caught pocketing the last cookie.

### Banned constructions (vague pointers that compare nothing)

"in a way that", "the way [noun] does when", "a kind of", "sort of", "something like", "the sort/kind of [noun] that", "had a quality/texture/weight to it". When tempted, state what physically happened.

### Mandatory grep check (before you consider a draft finished)

Run Grep on your own chapter for:
`in a way|the way |a kind of|kind of|sort of|something like|had a (quality|texture|weight)|the (sort|kind) of`
Judge each hit ("the way to the door" is fine). Fix every real one. Run it again until nothing real remains.

Then run Grep for the first-person tells:
`I (realized|couldn't help)|a wave of|breath I didn't|let out a breath`
Fix each hit.

Then Grep for `like a |like an |as if|as though| as [a-z]+ as ` to count your figures against the budget of three — this count includes dialogue. The grep misses forms like "hard as a doorknob", so also read for those, and list every figure you kept in your handoff note.

## 9. Length and padding

- The word-count figure is a rough range, not a quota. Never add material to reach it.
- When the beats the author described are done, stop. Say so in your handoff note if the chapter came in short.
- Padding to cut: restating what a scene just showed, the narrator explaining a feeling that was already clear, atmosphere that changes nothing, ending paragraphs on a summing-up line.

### Do not over-correct toward "style"

**A recurring failure mode in this project: adding small extra details, gestures, or flourishes that aren't in the author's paragraph, in an attempt to sound more like the target voice.** This is backwards. The rules in this document, applied plainly, are enough — they are not a license to decorate. If a beat, a gesture, a bit of sensory texture, or a stray thought isn't called for by the author's paragraph or genuinely required to make an existing beat make sense, it doesn't belong on the page. When in doubt, cut it rather than add it. A plainer draft that hits every real beat is better than a more "textured" one that invents beats nobody asked for.

### Essay tells (rewrite on sight)

- "I felt X because Y." Show what X made the narrator do.
- A beat stated once, then re-explained in a trailing clause ("He does X — the Y he'd never say out loud"). State the beat once and stop. If the beat doesn't land on its own, the fix is a better beat, not an explainer bolted onto it.
- A reflection that wraps up the scene's meaning.
- Abstract nouns where a concrete one would do ("a sense of unease", "a moment of understanding").
- "It wasn't X, it was Y" reversals more than once a chapter.
- A narrator explaining their own motives in full, tidy sentences.

## 10. Narrator voice cards

**Only Lucifer, Elizabeth, and Eve ever narrate a chapter.** Everyone else (Azrael, Sophie, James, Claire, Sam, and anyone else) is dialogue and behavior only — no voice card, no POV chapter, no exceptions without an explicit author decision first.

Fill these in with the author. What follows uses only what the project already establishes. Do not invent beyond it. Anything marked TODO is waiting on the author.

**Elizabeth**
- Skeptical questioner. Asks direct, pointed questions. Notices inconsistencies, evasions and gaps between said and shown. This is a learned habit and she can be wrong.
- Otherwise open and warm, not suspicious by default.
- Not calculating. She feels and acts more than she analyzes, gets distracted, lets things go, and makes small impulsive choices. Her pointed questions come in the moment. She does not scheme, tally or plan ahead in her head.
- Early on she genuinely hates Lucifer, and stays in his orbit because of what she reads as broken in him.

**Lucifer**
- Caustic, snide, sarcastic, angry. Aimed at everyone, Elizabeth included.
- Calls his brother **Azrael** — no alias, no human cover name for him anymore. (Lucifer himself is still called **Adrian** by humans who don't know the truth.)
- Early on his interior shows no warmth, attraction or liking toward Elizabeth.
- TODO: how his sarcasm sounds on the page; what he notices first; imagery; speech habits; what he never admits.

**Eve**
- Still loves Lucifer; doesn't register conscious remorse for the Fall, but her soul carries the weight of it, which is partly what landed her in Hell.
- Blames Lucifer for the situation even while still loving him; hates Elizabeth and goes after her to get Lucifer back.
- Speaks the divine language exactly once in the whole book, at the climax — never in her own narration otherwise.
